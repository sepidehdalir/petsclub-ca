Search for what a vet visit costs in Canada and you will find numbers. Almost none of them are traceable to anyone who sets a price, and that is not an accident of bad publishing — it is a structural feature of how veterinary medicine is organised in this country.

This article does not give you a price list. What it gives you is the thing that is actually portable: how a veterinary bill is assembled, why the same procedure legitimately costs different amounts at two clinics on the same street, and how to get a real figure for your own animal in your own city.

somewhere else would look more useful than this article and be considerably

</Note>

## Why nobody publishes it, which is a Canadian answer

There is a reason the number you want does not exist in a public document, and it is worth understanding because it explains the whole landscape.

Veterinary practices are individual businesses that set their own fees, and professional associations are constrained in what they can do about that. The Competition Bureau's guidance to trade associations is direct: professional associations should not create rules that set prices, mandate levels or types of services, restrict advertising, or exclude competitors. An association publishing a binding fee schedule for its members would be setting prices among competitors, which is the thing competition law exists to prevent.

The practical consequence for you is that **there is no Canadian equivalent of a published tariff for veterinary services**, there is not going to be one, and any figure presented as "the Canadian price" was assembled by someone with no authority to set it.

## What the federal picture actually shows

The Competition Bureau examined this sector directly, and its findings are the most authoritative Canadian material available on why costs behave the way they do.

**The market has consolidated.** In 2009, nearly all Canadian veterinary practices were independently owned. By the Bureau's account, corporate chains now hold around a fifth of the market — roughly 20.4% — with the largest consolidators named as Vet Strategy, National Veterinary Associates and VCA. Ownership is not something a clinic's signage tells you, and it is a fair question to ask.

**Several forces push costs up at once.** The Bureau identifies inflation, the surge in pet ownership during the pandemic, a shortage of veterinarians, and limited competition. That combination matters for expectation-setting: a bill that seems higher than it was five years ago probably is, and not because your clinic is doing something unusual.

**Canadians spend around $9.3 billion a year on veterinary services**, on the Bureau's figures — a number that says nothing about your invoice but a great deal about why this is a sector the federal competition regulator has looked at.

## The medication question, which differs by province

This is the most directly actionable Canadian fact in the article, and almost no pet owner knows it.

Pet medications in Canada are largely distributed exclusively to veterinarians, which means the person prescribing a drug is frequently also the person selling it. The Bureau has flagged this as a competition concern and has recommended that provinces require pet medications be supplied to pharmacists, so that a prescription can be filled elsewhere.

Where that stands is provincial. On the Bureau's account, regulations in **Ontario, British Columbia, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia** prohibit veterinarians from reselling medications to pharmacists — while **Quebec** is cited as the example of a province where the arrangement works differently and which the Bureau points to as a model.

What to do with that: **ask for a written prescription** rather than assuming you must buy the medication at the clinic, particularly for a long-term drug. Whether you can fill it elsewhere depends on where you live and on the drug, and asking costs nothing. Nothing here is a reason to change or delay a medication — that is a clinical decision — but it is a reason to ask a question most people never ask.

Two things follow that are worth internalising.

**An estimate is a range for a reason.** For anything where the plan can change once someone can see what is going on — a dental where radiographs reveal extractions, an exploratory procedure — the honest estimate has an upper and lower bound. A practice that gives you a single confident number for that kind of work is telling you less, not more.

**The expensive events are rarely the ones people budget for.** Routine care is predictable and comparatively modest. What breaks a budget is an unplanned hospitalisation, and that is the argument both the [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) cost guides make from the other direction.

## What actually drives the variation

- **Where you are.** Costs differ between provinces and between cities in the same province, and rural areas may have fewer clinics and a longer drive to emergency care.
- **The animal's weight.** Drug doses, anaesthetic, and much else scale with size. This is why a great dane and a chihuahua are not comparable patients on an invoice.
- **Routine versus emergency.** The same problem costs differently at eleven on a Tuesday morning and at two on a Sunday.
- **What is found.** Complexity discovered during a procedure legitimately changes the number.
- **Ownership and overheads.** Equipment on site means faster answers and a different cost base than referring out.
- **Whether it is a first visit.** A practice that already holds the history is not repeating work.

## The one price in this article

Municipal licence fees are published by the municipality that sets them, which is why they can appear here when veterinary fees cannot.

**Toronto's published pet licensing fees in 2026** are $25 for a spayed or neutered dog and $60 for one that is not, and $15 for a spayed or neutered cat against $50. Reduced and waived rates are available to seniors and to lower-income households.

Those are Toronto's fees and nobody else's — every municipality sets its own, and [what varies between Canadian cities](/guides/pet-licensing-across-canada) is more than the amount. It is included as a demonstration that the spay/neuter differential is real money recurring annually, which is a point the [spay and neuter guide](/guides/spaying-and-neutering-in-canada) makes and could not previously evidence.

## Getting a real number for your own city

The method both cost guides describe, extended for this one:

<Checklist title="An hour that produces a figure you can trust">

- Phone three practices near you and ask what a wellness examination costs. Clinics quote this routinely and it is the cleanest comparison available
- Ask what the vaccination series costs, and what spay or neuter costs for your animal's projected adult weight
- Ask what parasite prevention they would expect for your area, annually
- Ask what a dental costs, and specifically whether radiographs are included in that figure — the [dental guide](/guides/dental-care-for-dogs-and-cats) explains why that question separates two very different procedures
- Ask what the after-hours arrangement is and where you would be sent, then phone that hospital and ask what an emergency consultation costs
- Ask whether written estimates are provided before significant work, and whether payment is expected at the time of service
- Look up your own municipality's licence fee on the city's own page
- Get two or three insurance quotes for the breed, age and postal code, even if you decide against insuring — the premium is a market estimate of the risk you would otherwise carry yourself

</Checklist>

That produces a budget specific to your animal and your city, sourced by you, in about an hour. It is the only kind that is worth anything.

## Planning, rather than reacting

**Decide how an unplanned four-figure bill would be met, before it arrives.** Insurance, a dedicated savings account, or both — and the [insurance guide](/guides/pet-insurance-in-canada) covers why the exclusions decide more than the premium does, and why the cover available narrows with every entry in the animal's file.

**Say what your constraints are, out loud, early.** A veterinarian who knows the budget can tell you which diagnostics would change the plan and which are confirmatory, what a staged approach would look like, and what the realistic options are. That conversation works before decisions are made and not afterwards. It is a normal conversation in veterinary practice and owners consistently underestimate how willing clinics are to have it.

**Ask about payment arrangements before you need them**, including whether third-party financing is accepted, since [emergency care is generally payable at the time of service](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada).

## The short version

Nobody publishes what veterinary care costs in Canada because no authority sets it — practices set their own fees, and competition law constrains associations from doing it for them. Any national figure you find was assembled by someone without standing to set it.

What you can know is how a bill is built: consultation, diagnostics, procedure and anaesthesia, medication, hospitalisation, after-hours premium, tax. Knowing those lines is what makes an estimate readable and a question answerable.

Ask for a written prescription rather than assuming, because whether you can fill it elsewhere is a provincial question. Phone three clinics and build your own number. And decide how a four-figure night would be paid for on a day when nothing is wrong.
