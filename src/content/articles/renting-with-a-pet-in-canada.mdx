Finding a rental in Canada is hard. Finding one with a dog is harder, and most of the advice about it is either American or wrong. The rules genuinely differ from province to province — in one place a “no pets” clause in your lease may be unenforceable, and a few hours’ drive away the same clause is binding. Before you argue with a landlord, you need to know which country you are in, legally speaking.

This guide covers the two halves of the problem: establishing what the rules actually are where you live, and making yourself the tenant a landlord says yes to.

## First: the rules are provincial, and they are not similar

Residential tenancy law in Canada is provincial and territorial. There is no national rule about pets, and the differences are not cosmetic.

Broadly, provinces fall into three shapes:

- **Places where a no-pets term in a signed lease has limited or no force.** A landlord may still decline to rent to you in the first place, and there are usually carve-outs — condominium rules, another occupant’s serious allergy, or an animal that is genuinely dangerous.
- **Places where a no-pets clause is a normal, enforceable contract term.** If you sign it, it binds you, and breaching it is grounds for action.
- **Places where pets are broadly the landlord’s discretion**, sometimes with a separate pet damage deposit permitted on top of the security deposit.

Two things follow from this. The first is that anything you read on a forum about “they can’t do that” is only true in some provinces. The second is that a pet deposit or monthly pet fee that is entirely legal in one province may not be permitted in another — so before you pay one, confirm it is allowed where you are.

## Condominiums add a second rulebook

If the unit is in a condo, there are two sets of rules and the stricter one wins. The lease is between you and the landlord; the condominium’s declaration, bylaws and rules bind the unit itself and can restrict the number of pets, their size or weight, and sometimes their species.

A landlord cannot grant you something the corporation forbids. This catches people out constantly, because the landlord is often relaxed and the corporation is not.

**Ask for the pet provisions of the declaration and rules in writing before you sign anything.** “The other unit has a dog” is not evidence that dogs are allowed; it may be evidence that someone is about to receive a letter.

## Deposits, fees and what happens to your money

Whatever the arrangement is called, get three things clear in writing:

1. **Is it legal here?** Some provinces expressly permit a pet damage deposit and cap it; others do not permit any additional deposit at all. Non-refundable monthly “pet rent” is common in the United States and is not universally permitted here.
2. **Is it refundable, and against what?** A damage deposit is held against damage. It is not a fee for the privilege of having an animal, and it should come back if nothing is damaged.
3. **How is the condition of the unit being recorded?** Insist on a written, photographed move-in inspection, signed by both of you. This is the single most effective thing you can do to get a deposit back later, and it takes twenty minutes.

## Make yourself the easy yes

Landlords who refuse pets are rarely doing it out of dislike. They are managing risk they cannot assess: noise complaints, damage, and the possibility that the animal is unmanaged. Everything below is aimed at replacing an unknown with evidence.

### The pet résumé

One page, as a PDF, attached to your application. It sounds twee. It works, because it lets a landlord say yes with something on file.

- A clear photo, and the pet’s name, age, breed or type, and adult weight
- Spayed or neutered, and when
- Vaccination status and the clinic’s name — your veterinarian can provide a summary
- Municipal licence number, where your city licenses pets
- Training: name the class, the trainer or the organisation. “Completed group obedience at ——” is a fact; “very well behaved” is an opinion
- **What happens during the working day** — this is the question every landlord is actually asking. Crated, at daycare, a midday walker, someone home. Say which
- Any relevant history: a rescue’s background, a senior’s routine, a cat that is strictly indoors
- An offer to bring the pet to the viewing

### References

Two or three, with contact details and permission to call:

- A previous landlord, ideally one who saw the unit at move-out
- A neighbour from a previous building, on noise
- Your veterinarian, groomer or daycare, on temperament and handling

A previous landlord’s reference that specifically says the unit had no pet damage is worth more than everything else combined.

### Tenant insurance

Most tenant policies include personal liability, which is the part a landlord cares about. Two warnings: some insurers exclude or surcharge specific breeds, and some ask about bite history. **Answer accurately.** An inaccurate application is exactly the thing an insurer relies on to deny a claim later, which leaves you personally exposed for the incident the landlord was worried about.

Increasingly, landlords ask for proof of tenant insurance as a condition of the lease. Having it already, with liability coverage confirmed, removes one more reason to pick a different applicant.

## Put the pet in the lease

Even where pets are protected, ambiguity is what causes fights. Get an addendum, or a clause, that records:

- The specific animals — species, name, approximate weight. “One adult neutered male cat, indoor only” is unambiguous
- That the pet is permitted for the term of the tenancy
- What happens if you acquire another animal, and whether that needs consent
- Responsibility for damage beyond normal wear, and how it will be assessed
- Any building-specific conditions — leashed in common areas, service elevator only, no pets on the roof deck

A landlord who is willing to say yes verbally will usually sign this. One who will not is telling you something useful.

## Searching, realistically

- **Filter for pet-friendly, then verify.** Listing filters are unreliable, and “pet friendly” frequently means “one cat under ten pounds.”
- **Call rather than email.** A short phone conversation where you lead with the pet and immediately follow with the résumé and references converts far better than an email that gets filtered.
- **Be upfront, always.** Moving a pet in quietly is the fastest route to losing both the housing and the pet, and it destroys the reference you will need next time.
- **Smaller landlords are often more flexible** than large property managers, because they can exercise judgement rather than apply a policy.
- **Time it if you can.** Turnover in most Canadian rental markets is heaviest in late spring and summer, which is when there is enough supply for a pet-friendly unit to actually exist.
- **Be honest about size and breed.** Insurers and some condo rules turn on this, and a discovered misstatement ends the tenancy.

## If you are refused

What recourse you have depends entirely on where you are. In a province where no-pets terms have limited force, a refusal *after* you have signed is a different matter from a refusal at the application stage — and the application stage is the harder one to challenge anywhere.

The place to ask is your provincial or territorial tenancy authority, and where you need actual advice, a tenant advocacy or legal clinic in your province. Do not take a position with your landlord on the strength of a comment thread.

## Where to check

These are the bodies that publish the actual rules and answer questions about them. Start with your own province or territory.

- **British Columbia** — Residential Tenancy Branch
- **Alberta** — Service Alberta, Residential Tenancies
- **Saskatchewan** — Office of Residential Tenancies
- **Manitoba** — Residential Tenancies Branch
- **Ontario** — Landlord and Tenant Board (Tribunals Ontario)
- **Quebec** — Tribunal administratif du logement
- **New Brunswick** — Residential Tenancies Tribunal
- **Nova Scotia** — Residential Tenancy Program, Service Nova Scotia
- **Prince Edward Island** — Island Regulatory and Appeals Commission
- **Newfoundland and Labrador** — Residential Tenancies, Digital Government and Service NL
- **Yukon, Northwest Territories, Nunavut** — each territory’s Residential Tenancies Office

For the condominium side, the corporation’s declaration, bylaws and rules are obtained through the landlord or the property manager, and in some provinces through a status or estoppel certificate.

- Confirmed the pet rules for your province with the tenancy authority, not a forum
- Read the condo declaration and rules, if it is a condo
- Confirmed any deposit or fee is permitted where you live, and refundable
- Completed a written, photographed move-in inspection, signed by both parties
- Pet named and permitted in the lease or a signed addendum
- Tenant insurance in place, with liability confirmed and the pet accurately declared
- Municipal pet licence transferred to the new address

## The short version

Establish the law where you are before you negotiate, read the condo rules before you sign, and turn the landlord’s unknown into a one-page document with references attached. Most refusals are risk management. Give them something to manage the risk with.
