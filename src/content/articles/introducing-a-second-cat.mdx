The single most common way to create a permanent problem between two cats is to be in a hurry on the first day.

It is an understandable hurry. The new cat is here, the resident cat is right there, and the instinct is to let them meet and get it over with. What happens instead is that both cats learn something about each other in the first ten minutes that then takes months to unlearn — and in a lot of households it never quite gets unlearned, and the two cats simply spend a decade avoiding one another in a way the humans stop noticing.

Done properly this takes weeks, most of which are boring. That is the whole method.

## Why cats are not dogs about this

Dogs are a social species that forms groups. Cats form social groups too, but on their own terms and by their own rules, and the rule that matters here is that **cats identify group membership by smell.**

A cat that smells like you and the sofa and the other cat is in the group. A cat that smells like somewhere else is an intruder in a territory that is already spoken for. That is not a personality judgement and no amount of the two of them being "lovely with people" changes it.

Which means the introduction is not really about getting them to like each other. It is about getting them to smell like one household before they ever have to look at each other.

## First, the question that precedes all of this

Households acquire a second cat far more readily than a second dog, and frequently without a decision having been made at all — a stray turned up, a litter needed homes, the first cat seemed lonely.

That last one is worth examining, because it is the most common reason given and the least reliable. Cats are not obligate social animals in the way dogs are. Some genuinely do better with company; plenty are entirely content as the only cat and would experience a housemate as a permanent intrusion into a territory they already hold. A cat that is under-stimulated is not the same as a cat that is lonely, and the fix for the first one is [enrichment](/guides/indoor-cat-enrichment-canadian-homes) rather than another cat.

Two questions worth answering honestly before you commit:

**Does your house have room in the sense that matters?** Not floor area — resources and routes. If you cannot fit another litter box in a separate location, another feeding station in another room, and a way for a cat to cross a room without touching the floor, the space is the constraint rather than the cats.

**Does your budget model two?** Almost every recurring line doubles, litter scales directly, and when something is wrong you frequently end up investigating both animals because you cannot tell which one has the problem. The [cat cost guide](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) calls the number of cats the variable that dominates everything else, and the one most likely to change without anyone deciding.

If the answers are yes, the rest of this article is how to do it without creating a decade of quiet hostility.

## Before the new cat arrives

Set up a room the new cat will own completely — a spare bedroom, an office, a bathroom at a pinch. It needs its own litter box, food and water separated from each other, somewhere enclosed to hide, something to climb, a scratching post and a bed. The [first thirty days with a kitten](/guides/bringing-home-a-kitten-first-30-days) covers that setup in detail, and it applies to an adult cat too.

Then look at the rest of the house through your resident cat's eyes and count. The rule that prevents most multi-cat friction is boring and it is **one per cat plus one**, applied to litter boxes, water sources, feeding stations, beds and scratching posts — and placed in *different locations* rather than lined up in a row, so that one cat cannot sit in a doorway and control all of them at once.

Vertical routes matter here more than floor space, because height is how cats avoid each other without confrontation. A house with no way to get up and across is a house with only one option when two cats want the same corridor. The [enrichment guide](/guides/indoor-cat-enrichment-canadian-homes) covers building those routes.

## The thing to stop doing: waiting for a fight

Cat conflict is mostly silent, and that is why owners miss it for years.

Two cats that scream and swipe at each other are having an obvious disagreement. Far more common is a household where one cat has quietly won, and the signs are all about access rather than aggression:

- One cat sits in a doorway, on the stairs, or beside the litter box, and the other changes route
- A long, unbroken stare from across the room, which the other cat breaks off
- One cat that has stopped using a particular part of the house
- One cat that only eats when the other is elsewhere
- Sudden litter box avoidance, over-grooming, or a cat that has become withdrawn
- A cat that has started sleeping somewhere odd, high up, or behind something

None of that looks like fighting. All of it is a cat telling you the resource distribution in your house is wrong, and the answer is usually more boxes, more feeding stations, more vertical escape routes, and more separation — not more forced togetherness.

<VetNote>
Changes in litter box habits, appetite, weight, grooming or activity are not
automatically behavioural. They are also common early signs of medical
problems, and cats are unusually good at concealing illness — so a cat that
starts avoiding the box or withdrawing during an introduction needs a
veterinary appointment rather than an assumption that it is protesting. If
either cat is injured in a fight, bite wounds in cats abscess readily and are
worth a call rather than a wait. Persistent, serious inter-cat aggression is
a recognised behavioural problem with real treatment options; start with your
own clinic, which can rule out medical contributors and refer you onward.
This describes what to look for so you know when to call; it does not
diagnose or treat anything.

## Cats and dogs are a different project

The same principle — scent and gradual exposure before contact — but the failure mode is not the same. With two cats the risk is a slow, silent stalemate. With a dog and a cat the risk is one bad moment.

What changes:

- **The dog needs to be able to do something before you start.** A dog that will settle on a mat and disengage on cue is a dog you can manage during an introduction. One that cannot is not ready, and that is a training project first.
- **The dog is on a lead for every early session**, indoors, with you holding it.
- **The cat must have escape routes the dog cannot follow** — height, a gap the dog cannot fit through, a room with a cat-sized opening. The cat should never be cornered, and it should never be held.
- **The cat sets the pace.** A cat that runs will be chased by a great many otherwise well-behaved dogs, and one chase can undo weeks.
- **Feed separately, permanently**, and put the litter box somewhere the dog physically cannot get to. This is not a training matter; it is a plumbing matter.

## How long it actually takes

Some pairs are fine in a fortnight. Some take three months. A minority never become friends and instead reach a workable indifference, which is a perfectly good outcome and is what a lot of successful multi-cat households actually look like.

What is not a good outcome is a household where one cat has stopped using half the house. If you are months in and that is where you are, the environment usually needs changing more than the cats do.

<Checklist title="Before you bring the second one home">

- A separate room, fully set up, that the new cat owns completely
- Litter boxes at one per cat plus one, in separate locations, none of them in a dead end
- Separate feeding stations in different rooms — not two bowls side by side
- Vertical routes so a cat can leave a room without walking past another cat
- Somewhere enclosed to hide in more than one room
- A plan for scent swapping before anyone sees anyone
- A barrier for stage four that cannot be rushed through
- An honest answer to whether your budget covers two — [it is the variable that dominates](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada)
- Both animals up to date on parasite prevention before they share a house

</Checklist>

## The short version

Cats decide who is family by smell, so let them smell each other for a week or two before they ever see each other. Swap bedding. Swap territory. Then sight through a barrier, then short supervised sessions, then time.

Count your resources and multiply them — one per cat plus one, in separate places — because most inter-cat trouble is a competition problem wearing a personality problem's clothing.

And watch for the quiet version. A cat blocking a doorway is doing something. A cat that no longer goes upstairs has already lost.
