Keeping a cat indoors is a reasonable decision, and in most of urban Canada it is the one most veterinarians will steer you toward. It also comes with a bill attached, and the bill is not money. It is that everything the outdoors used to supply — height, novelty, things to stalk, somewhere to scratch, somewhere to hide — now has to come from inside your house.

For roughly half the Canadian year that house is also closed up, dark by five, and dry as a cracker. This is what to actually do about it.

If you are still weighing the decision itself, [indoor, outdoor or in between](/guides/indoor-or-outdoor-cats-in-canada) covers the trade rather than the implementation.

## The five things a cat needs to be able to do

Not a personality theory. Just the behaviours cats perform when they have the option, and which an indoor home has to make room for:

- **Hunt** — stalk, chase, pounce, catch.
- **Climb and survey** — get high, and look down at things.
- **Scratch** — condition claws and mark, on a surface that gives resistance.
- **Hide** — withdraw somewhere enclosed.
- **Patrol** — move around a territory on a route of its own choosing.

Almost every enrichment idea worth doing is a way of supplying one of those five. Almost every indoor cat problem is one of them having nowhere to go.

## Vertical space is the cheapest thing you can buy

If you do one thing, do this one. A cat uses the vertical dimension the way we use floor plan, and most homes give it none.

The goal is **routes, not perches**: a cat should be able to cross a room without touching the floor. Cat trees, wall shelves, the top of a bookcase with something to step onto, the back of a sofa to a windowsill to a cabinet.

This matters disproportionately in Canadian cities, where a lot of us are in apartments and condos. You cannot add floor area to a one-bedroom. You can add three levels to it in an afternoon with a drill and two shelves, and the cat experiences that as a bigger territory.

In a multi-cat home, height is also how cats avoid each other without conflict. A tense household with no vertical escape route is a household with one route: the confrontation.

## The window is the television

A window perch at the right height, in a window that has something happening outside it, does more for an indoor cat than most purchased toys.

Two Canadian-specific notes:

- **Follow the sun through the year.** The angle in January is nothing like July, and the perch that was perfect in summer may sit in shade all winter. Move it.
- **If you put a bird feeder outside the window**, position matters for the birds. Feeders very close to the glass or well away from it are both safer than the middle distance, where birds build up enough speed to be hurt by a collision. Worth getting right — the entertainment is not supposed to cost anything.

## Hunting: two sessions a day, done properly

This is the highest-yield thing most owners are not doing, and the way it is usually done makes it much less effective than it could be.

**Use a wand toy**, not your hands, and never your hands. Move it **like prey**: away from the cat, around a corner, under a blanket, pausing where it can’t quite be seen. Prey does not fly at a cat’s face; it tries to escape and it hides. A toy waved in front of a cat gets a swipe. A toy that disappears behind a cushion gets a hunt.

**Let the cat catch it** at the end. A hunt that never succeeds is frustrating rather than satisfying.

**Then feed.** Hunt, catch, eat, groom, sleep is the sequence a cat runs on, and following play with a meal completes it. Two sessions a day of five to ten minutes, one of them in the evening, does more than an hour of leaving toys on the floor.

**Rotate and store.** Keep three or four toys in circulation and the rest in a cupboard. A toy that has been on the rug for a month is not a toy, it is decor.

## Make food into an activity

A bowl of food is a thirty-second event. The same food can be twenty minutes.

Puzzle feeders, treat balls, food scattered across a room, or simply portioning a meal into three or four bowls in different places all work. If your cat has never done it, start easy — a puzzle that is too hard just gets abandoned.

How much a cat should be eating, and in what form, is a question for your veterinarian rather than for the internet, and it matters more than most owners realise once a cat is indoors full-time.

## Scratching: the right object, in the right place

Scratching is not optional behaviour and it is not misbehaviour. The only question is what surface gets used.

- **Tall enough to stretch out fully.** Most posts sold are too short. A cat wants to reach up and pull.
- **Sturdy enough not to wobble.** A post that moves gets abandoned once, permanently.
- **Both orientations.** Some cats are vertical scratchers, some horizontal. Offer both; cardboard is cheap.
- **Placed where the cat wants to scratch** — beside sleeping spots, near doorways and at the entrance to a room. A beautiful post in the basement is furniture. An ugly one next to the sofa is a solution.
- **Multiple, in a multi-cat home.** One per cat plus one, same as boxes.

Declawing is prohibited by the veterinary regulators in a number of Canadian provinces, and it is not the answer to a scratching problem anywhere. If your cat is scratching furniture, the fix is a better post in a better place, plus regular claw trimming — which is much easier if you started handling paws when they were a kitten, as the [first thirty days guide](/guides/bringing-home-a-kitten-first-30-days) sets out.

## Hiding places are enrichment too

It sounds backwards, but somewhere to withdraw to is part of a well-set-up home. Cats use enclosed spaces to cope with things they find unsettling — the vacuum, visitors, a delivery, another cat.

Cardboard boxes, a covered bed, a shelf in a wardrobe left ajar, the space under a bed. Free, and one of the most reliably used things in the house.

## Winter, specifically

Roughly half the Canadian year needs its own plan.

- **Everything is drier.** Heated indoor air means more static, flakier skin and more shedding. Brushing more often through the winter helps, and it is also handling practice.
- **The light disappears.** Cats sleep more and move less. Keep the two play sessions consistent even when nobody feels like it — the evening one especially, because that is when an under-stimulated cat starts running the hallway at 3 a.m.
- **The holidays are the hazard season.** Lilies are seriously toxic to cats and arrive in bouquets. Tinsel, ribbon and thread can cause serious internal injury if swallowed. Doors get propped open with guests coming and going. Wrap the season’s risks up before the guests arrive rather than during.
- **Windows and balconies.** Screens do not hold cats, and this is worth re-checking each spring when the windows go back open.

## Multi-cat homes need resources, not just space

The rule that prevents most multi-cat friction is boring: **one per cat, plus one**, applied to litter boxes, water sources, beds, perches and scratching posts — and placed in different locations rather than lined up in a row, so one cat cannot control all of them at once.

Separate feeding stations, in different rooms if you can. Vertical routes so a cat can leave a room without walking past another cat. Most “cats fighting” is two cats with one resource between them.

## Catios, harnesses and supervised time out

None of this is an argument against outdoor access on your terms. A catio, harness walks, a cat-proofed fence or a supervised yard session all add something no indoor setup replicates, and they carry very little of the risk of free roaming. That is covered properly in the [indoor and outdoor guide](/guides/indoor-or-outdoor-cats-in-canada).

## Ten minutes a week

Enrichment fails when it becomes a project. Make it a weekly habit instead:

- Rotate the toys — three out, three away.
- Move one perch or one piece of furniture the cat uses.
- Put out a new cardboard box and throw away last week’s.
- Hide the dry portion of a meal in three places instead of one.
- Check the scratching posts for wobble, and replace the cardboard when it’s shredded.

Novelty is the point. The same excellent setup, unchanged for a year, stops being interesting.

## The short version

Height, a window, a proper hunt twice a day, something worth scratching, and somewhere to hide. Everything else on the market is a variation on those five.

Indoors is a good decision for most Canadian cats. It is also a commitment to being the environment, which is a fair trade — and considerably cheaper than it sounds, since the three most effective items on this list are a shelf, a cardboard box and ten minutes with a stick.
