Emergencies are not made worse by the emergency. They are made worse by the twenty minutes spent searching for an address, finding the carrier, and working out whether the car has enough fuel — while an animal deteriorates on the kitchen floor.

Almost all of that is solvable in advance, on an ordinary evening, in under an hour. This guide is about doing that preparation. It is deliberately not a symptom checker: deciding what is wrong with an animal is veterinary work, done by someone who can examine it, and no list on a website is a substitute for a phone call to a clinic.

## Decide where you would go, before you need to know

The first job is a name and an address, not a search.

Ask your own veterinary practice what its after-hours arrangement is — some run their own service, most refer to a dedicated emergency hospital, and in many parts of the country the nearest overnight option is a real drive away. If you have not yet settled on a regular practice, the [guide to choosing one](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada) covers that first, because the emergency plan follows from it.

Then confirm the details yourself rather than trusting a search result: emergency hours change, services close, and a listing can be years out of date. Phone the emergency hospital once, in daylight, and check that it is still operating, still taking your species, and still at that address.

Write down, for the fridge and for your phone:

- Your regular clinic — name, phone, address, hours
- The after-hours or emergency destination — name, phone, address, hours
- The drive time, in winter, at the worst hour
- A second option, if there is one
- An animal poison control service, if you decide to use one

The same exercise has to be repeated anywhere you take the animal for more than a night — the folder travels, the destinations do not. [Travelling with a pet](/guides/travelling-with-a-pet-in-canada) covers building the second set.

<Note title="Phone ahead when you can">
Calling on the way is not a formality. It lets the hospital prepare for what is
arriving, confirms they can take the case rather than sending you further on,
and gives you instructions for the journey. If someone else can drive, make the
call from the passenger seat.
</Note>

## The transport plan

Getting there is the part people have not thought about.

**Cats and small animals need a carrier, and the carrier needs to be findable.** A carrier stored in a basement behind the Christmas decorations is a carrier you will not have in ten minutes. Leaving it out permanently, open, with bedding in it, does two jobs at once: it is always to hand, and it stops being an object your cat associates only with bad days. That habit is worth starting on [day one with a kitten](/guides/bringing-home-a-kitten-first-30-days).

**A dog needs a leash and collar or harness within reach of the door**, and for a large dog, a plan for the moment it cannot walk. A blanket used as a stretcher works, and it works far better with two people than one. Think about who the second person is.

**If you do not drive**, sort this out now. Which taxi or rideshare services in your city carry animals, under what conditions, and do they require a carrier? Is there a neighbour, friend or family member who would take the call at three in the morning? Ask them before you need to.

**Fuel, or a charge, and a route.** Keeping the tank above a quarter through the winter is a small habit that occasionally matters a great deal.

**An injured or frightened animal may bite or scratch**, including one that has never bitten anyone. This is pain and fear, not character, and the standing veterinary advice is to protect yourself first. Handle the minimum necessary and support the body rather than pulling at a limb. A towel wrapped round a cat limits movement and is usually enough. Muzzling a dog is sometimes appropriate and sometimes actively dangerous — never on a dog with a chest injury, never on a flat-faced breed, and never left on unattended — so treat it as a question for the clinic on the phone rather than a default.

## What to bring

Assembled in advance and kept in one folder or one note on your phone, this is fifteen minutes of work that saves fifteen minutes when they are expensive.

<Checklist title="The emergency folder">

- Your animal’s name, age, breed or type, and approximate weight
- Microchip number, and the registry it is with
- Your regular clinic’s name and phone number
- Vaccination and medical history — a printed summary or a photo of the record
- **A current medication list**: every drug, the strength, the dose, and when the last one was given. Include supplements and parasite preventatives
- Known allergies and previous reactions
- Any chronic diagnoses, in plain words
- Insurance policy number, or the details of whatever you would use to pay
- The carrier, leash, harness and a blanket, all stored where you can reach them in the dark

</Checklist>

If something has been ingested, **bring the packaging** — the container, the label, the wrapper, the plant, whatever is left of it. The product name and concentration are frequently the difference between a specific treatment and a cautious one. If the animal has vomited and you can bring a sample safely, that can help too, though not at the cost of delay.

**Bring the timeline.** When did this start, what has changed since, what has the animal eaten, drunk, passed or brought up, and what if anything did you give it. Write it in the car if you have to. Under stress, people reliably compress six hours into “this morning”, and the sequence is part of the diagnosis.

**Photographs and video are genuinely useful**, particularly for anything intermittent. An episode that has stopped by the time you arrive is very hard to describe and very easy to show.

## Poisons and exposures

Suspected poisoning is one of the few situations where the response is unambiguous: **do not wait to see whether it settles, and do not induce vomiting unless a veterinary professional has told you to.**

That second half is not caution for its own sake. With corrosive substances, making an animal vomit brings the corrosive back up through tissue it has already burned. With petroleum distillates and other volatile hydrocarbons — fuel, solvents, lamp oil — the danger is aspiration into the lungs, which turns a poisoning into a pneumonia. The right answer depends on what was swallowed, how long ago, and the animal itself, and that is a judgement for someone qualified to make it.

Contact your veterinarian or an emergency hospital immediately. Animal poison control services also exist and can be reached by phone from Canada; they charge a consultation fee per incident, and a clinic may ask you to call one so they can work from a case number. Ask what the fee is when you call — it changes. Have the product in your hand.

The same applies to skin and eye exposures, to anything inhaled, and to insect or animal bites where you are unsure. And it applies to the seasonal ones — antifreeze in particular is covered in the [winter care guide](/guides/winter-dog-care-in-canada), and it is an absolute rather than a judgement call.

## Money, before the day arrives

Emergency care is expensive, it is usually unplanned, and payment is generally expected at the time of service. This is the part people are least prepared for, and it is the part that can force a decision nobody wants to make.

Sort out in advance:

- **Where the money would come from.** A dedicated savings buffer, a credit facility with room on it, insurance, or a combination. Whatever it is, know the answer before the night it matters. The [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) cost guides both make the same argument: the monthly cost is not what causes the crisis.
- **How your insurance actually pays.** Most policies reimburse you after you have settled the bill, which means you still need the funds on the day. Whether direct payment to a clinic is possible at all is a policy-by-policy question — the [insurance guide](/guides/pet-insurance-in-canada) covers what to check before you buy.
- **What the hospital accepts.** Payment methods, whether a deposit is requested on admission, and whether any third-party financing is accepted. One phone call, asked in daylight.

At the hospital, ask for a written estimate and ask what it covers. It is entirely reasonable to say what you can manage and ask what that buys — which diagnostics change the plan, which can wait, and what the alternatives are. Veterinary teams have this conversation constantly. Having it early, out loud, gets a better outcome than having it after the fact.

## What happens when you arrive

Emergency hospitals triage. The order of treatment is by severity, not by arrival, so a long wait can mean your animal is stable — and someone who arrives after you may be taken straight through. This is the system working, and it is much easier to accept if you know it in advance.

One Canadian point worth having settled beforehand: the veterinarian treating your animal at two in the morning is licensed by your province's regulator, not by a national body. The [guide to choosing a practice](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada) names them — the College of Veterinarians of Ontario, the College of Veterinarians of British Columbia, the Ordre des médecins vétérinaires du Québec, and their equivalents elsewhere. You will almost certainly never need this. But if something goes wrong and you want to raise a professional standards concern afterwards rather than a billing dispute, that regulator is the destination, and the process is provincial rather than uniform across the country.

Expect to be separated from your animal for assessment, to be asked the same questions more than once by different people, and to be given options rather than a single instruction. Bring a phone charger. Bring the folder.

## When uncertainty is itself the answer

The honest guidance about deciding at home is that you often cannot, and that the attempt is what costs time.

**If you are asking yourself whether this is an emergency, that question is the reason to phone.** Telephone triage is a normal, expected part of what veterinary practices and emergency hospitals do. Describing what you are seeing to someone qualified to weigh it takes a few minutes, costs little or nothing, and produces one of three answers: come now, come in the morning, or here is what to watch for. All three are better than guessing.

Some situations are ones owners commonly talk themselves out of and should not. The American Veterinary Medical Association publishes a list of the emergencies that warrant immediate veterinary consultation, and it is worth knowing before you need it:

- choking, difficulty breathing, or non-stop coughing and gagging
- bleeding that will not stop, or bleeding from the nose, mouth or rectum, coughing up blood, or blood in the urine
- inability to urinate or pass stool, or obvious pain when doing either
- suspected ingestion of something poisonous
- seizures, or staggering
- fractures, severe lameness, or an inability to use a leg
- obvious pain, or extreme anxiety
- heat stress or heatstroke
- severe vomiting or diarrhoea — more than two episodes in 24 hours, or either one alongside obvious illness
- refusal to drink for 24 hours or more
- eye injuries
- unconsciousness

To which one more, specific to deep-chested dogs: a swollen or distended belly with retching that brings nothing up. That combination can mean gastric dilatation-volvulus, and it is time-critical.

Phone rather than wait any of these out.

Two smaller notes that come up constantly. Very young animals — an unwell puppy or kitten — have far less reserve than an adult and deteriorate faster, so the threshold for calling should be lower. The same applies at the [other end of life](/guides/senior-dogs-and-cats), where a change is likelier to mean something and likelier to be dismissed. And a change in an animal you know well is worth taking seriously even when you cannot name what is different; cats in particular conceal illness, which is discussed in the [cat cost guide](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada).

## Do it tonight

Print the destinations and put them on the fridge. Put the same details in your phone. Write the medication list. Move the carrier somewhere you can reach it in the dark, and leave it open. Check the fuel.

None of that takes an hour, and all of it is only useful if it is done before the evening it is needed. If you want to compare notes on how it has gone for other people, the [vet costs](/community/vet-costs) and [pet insurance](/community/pet-insurance) categories in the community are where those conversations happen.
