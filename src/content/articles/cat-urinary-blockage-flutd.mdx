If you are reading this because your cat is in the litter box straining and producing nothing, stop and phone a veterinary clinic now. That is the only advice on this page that matters in the next hour, and everything below is written for the calmer day before or after.

A complete urinary obstruction is one of the small number of genuine emergencies in cat ownership where the window is measured in hours rather than days. Cornell's feline health centre states it plainly: the time from complete urinary obstruction until death may be **less than twenty-four to forty-eight hours**.

<VetNote>
Contact a veterinary clinic or emergency hospital **immediately** — not at the
next appointment — if a cat is straining in the litter box and producing little
or no urine, going in and out of the box repeatedly, crying out while trying to
urinate, licking persistently at the genital area, or has become lethargic, is
vomiting, or is off its food alongside any of that. A hard, painful abdomen is
a further reason to go now. This combination in a male cat in particular can
mean the urethra is obstructed, which is life-threatening within a short time.
Do not wait overnight, do not wait to see whether it settles, and do not
attempt anything at home. There is no home treatment for a suspected urinary
obstruction, and delay is the thing that changes the outcome. This describes
what to look for so you know when to call; it does not diagnose or treat
anything.
</VetNote>

## FLUTD is a category, not a diagnosis

This is the part that most owner-facing writing gets wrong, and it matters because the treatments differ completely.

**Feline lower urinary tract disease** is an umbrella term for a range of disorders affecting the bladder and urethra. Cornell describes it as covering conditions from mild to serious. What sits underneath it includes:

- **Feline idiopathic cystitis** — painful bladder inflammation with no identifiable underlying cause, in which stress plays a recognised part. This is the most common finding in cats presenting with lower urinary tract signs.
- **Urolithiasis** — urinary stones, which can irritate the bladder and can also physically obstruct the urethra.
- **Urethral obstruction** — a blockage of the urinary tract, which is the emergency, and which FLUTD is the leading cause of in male cats.
- **Infection**, which is less common in young cats than owners assume and more relevant in older ones.
- Anatomical abnormalities and, less commonly, tumours.

So "my cat has FLUTD" is closer to "my cat has a chest problem" than to a diagnosis. Which of these it is determines whether the answer is pain relief and environmental change, a prescription diet, surgery, or an emergency admission — and telling them apart requires examination and testing rather than a list of signs.

## The signs, and the line between urgent and emergency

Lower urinary tract signs look broadly similar whatever is causing them. Cornell lists difficult or painful urination, increased frequency, crying out while urinating, and blood in the urine, along with urinating outside the box and excessive licking of the genital area.

The distinction that decides your next hour is not *which* signs, but whether urine is coming out.

**Owners routinely misread obstruction as constipation.** A cat straining in the box, hunched, producing nothing, looks exactly like a cat that is constipated — and the treatment for one is unhurried while the other is measured in hours. If you are not certain which you are looking at, that uncertainty is the reason to phone rather than to wait and see.

## Which cats are at higher risk

Cornell notes that urethral obstruction occurs more frequently in **male and neutered male cats**, because the urethra is longer and narrower than a female's. It also reports the disease occurring more often in cats that are middle-aged, overweight, indoor, and have low activity levels.

That last cluster is worth sitting with, because three of the four are things a household influences. It is not a guarantee in either direction — plenty of lean active cats develop lower urinary tract disease — but it is a reason that weight and activity are treated as health matters rather than cosmetic ones.

## What happens at the clinic

Knowing the shape of it makes the decision to go easier, and makes the consent conversation less bewildering.

Expect an examination that includes feeling the bladder, which is often how an obstruction is first identified. Expect a discussion of diagnostics — urine testing, bloodwork to assess the systemic effects, and imaging to look for stones. For a confirmed obstruction, treatment typically involves relieving the blockage under sedation or anaesthesia, usually by passing a urinary catheter, alongside fluids and pain relief, and usually with hospitalisation for a period afterwards.

This is not a cheap night, and it is worth knowing that in advance rather than discovering it at the counter. Both cost guides name urinary blockage among the things a cat budget should be able to absorb, and the [cat cost guide](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) calls it the single most common reason a comfortable budget stops being comfortable. If cost is a real constraint, say so plainly and early — a veterinarian who knows the limits can tell you which diagnostics change the plan.

Two things that make the night go better and are arranged in advance: knowing where your [after-hours destination](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada) is before you need it, and having a practice that already holds your cat's history, which is what [choosing one early](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada) buys you.

## Reducing the risk, honestly framed

None of this prevents disease reliably, and it is not a substitute for veterinary care. It is what is generally recommended alongside it, and the reasoning is straightforward.

**Water intake.** More dilute urine is the consistent theme in managing several of these conditions. Multiple water sources in different rooms, wide shallow bowls, moving water if the cat prefers it, and — a question for your veterinarian rather than a default — whether wet food is appropriate for this cat.

**Weight and activity**, given the risk profile above. Two short play sessions a day is the same recommendation the [enrichment guide](/guides/indoor-cat-enrichment-canadian-homes) makes for other reasons.

**Stress, which is a genuine clinical factor rather than a soft one**, given the recognised role of stress in feline idiopathic cystitis. Predictable routine, somewhere to hide, vertical space, and resources that do not have to be contested. In a multi-cat house that means one per cat plus one, in separate places — and if there is friction between cats, the [second-cat guide](/guides/introducing-a-second-cat) covers reducing it.

**Litter box access**, because a cat that avoids the box for any reason holds urine longer. The [house-soiling guide](/guides/litter-box-problems-in-cats) covers the audit.

**Prescription diets** exist for several of these conditions and are genuinely effective for the ones they are formulated for — which is exactly why choosing one without a diagnosis is not a good idea. That is a conversation with a veterinarian who knows what you are treating.

## After an episode

Recurrence is a real feature of several of these conditions rather than a sign that something went wrong, and cats that have obstructed once can obstruct again. Ask directly what the plan is: what to watch for, what would justify another emergency visit, whether a diet change is recommended, and when to come back.

Then make sure everyone in the house knows the signs, not just the person who took the cat in.

## The short version

FLUTD is a category covering several different conditions, and the one that determines urgency is obstruction. Straining while producing little or no urine is an emergency at any hour — phone rather than wait, and do not attempt anything at home.

Male and neutered male cats are at higher risk; middle-aged, overweight, indoor and inactive cats appear more often. Water, weight, activity, stress and easy box access are the levers a household actually has, and none of them replaces a veterinary assessment.

If you cannot tell whether your cat is constipated or obstructed, that is the reason to make the call.
