The first hour is worth more than the next three days, and most of it gets spent on the wrong things.

People drive. They post on social media. They walk the neighbourhood calling the animal's name — which, for a frightened cat, is close to the least effective thing available. Meanwhile the two actions that most often actually reunite an animal with its household take about fifteen minutes and almost nobody does them first.

This is what to do, in order, and what to have already done before it happens.

## Do these two things first

**Update the microchip registration before you do anything else.** Not the chip — the record attached to it. If you have moved, changed phone number, or acquired the animal from a rescue or breeder who registered it in their own name, the number scans and points somewhere useless. This is the single most common failure in the entire recovery chain, and it is fixable from your phone in ten minutes while somebody else starts searching.

**File a lost report with your municipal animal services.** This is the step people skip because it feels bureaucratic. It is not. Municipal animal control and shelters are where found animals actually arrive, they take lost reports specifically so they can match them, and the report is what connects your animal to the person who picks it up on the other side of the neighbourhood.

Then keep going.

## The thing about microchips nobody tells you

A microchip is not a tracker. It stores a number, it works only when somebody scans it, and — this is the part worth knowing — **not every scanner reads every chip.**

The Canadian Veterinary Medical Association is explicit about this: there are three different RFID frequencies in use, and detection scanners are not all completely effective at reading all microchips. Its practical advice follows directly from that, which is to use **all** the search methods available to you rather than relying on the chip.

The CVMA also recommends having implanted chips scanned annually, usually at a routine health examination, to confirm they still work and have not migrated. That is a thirty-second request at an appointment you were already attending, and it is the difference between a chip that works and a chip you assume works.

So: the chip is one layer. A collar with a visible tag carrying a mobile number is another, and it is the one that gets an animal home without anyone visiting a clinic at all. Municipal licence tags are a third — several Canadian cities issue a numbered tag precisely so that a found animal can be traced, and [licensing rules differ by city](/guides/pet-licensing-across-canada).

## Cats and dogs go missing differently

This is the distinction that changes the search, and treating both the same is why a lot of cat searches fail.

**A lost dog travels.** It keeps moving, often in a straight line, often surprisingly far, and it is likely to be seen by people. Searching outward, alerting people over a wide radius, and getting the description in front of anyone driving is proportionate.

**A lost indoor cat usually does not.** A frightened cat that has got out typically hides, close by, silently, and stays put — often for days, often within a few properties of home. It will not come when called, because a terrified cat's strategy is to be undetectable, and that includes to you.

Which means the cat search is a physical, slow, ground-level search of hiding places rather than a wide sweep: under decks and porches, in sheds and garages, under vehicles, in crawl spaces, in dense shrubs, behind stacked wood. Ask neighbours for permission to look under their decks and in their garages — not to keep an eye out, but to let you physically check. Go at night when it is quiet, take a torch, and look for eyeshine at ground level.

And put something outside that smells of home near the door the cat left from.

## The search, in order

<Checklist title="The first twenty-four hours">

- Update the microchip registry — name, address, phone, and a second contact
- File a lost report with municipal animal services, with the description, licence number and chip number
- Search your own home first, thoroughly, including cupboards, behind appliances and inside anything that closes. Cats in particular are found indoors more often than owners expect
- Search the immediate ground level outdoors — under decks, porches, sheds, vehicles — before widening
- Ask immediate neighbours for permission to physically check their property, not just to watch for the animal
- Contact local shelters, humane societies and veterinary clinics, and give them the chip number
- Post clear photographs showing distinctive markings, with the area and your phone number
- Put up physical signs at intersections and on the routes people actually walk
- Leave familiar-smelling bedding, a litter tray or the carrier outside the door the animal left from
- Go out again after dark with a torch, quietly, and listen rather than call

</Checklist>

## Then keep checking in person

Reports and phone calls are not enough on their own, and this is where recoveries are most often lost.

**Visit the shelters in person, and keep visiting.** A description over the phone is only as good as the match somebody else makes from it, and animals arrive looking nothing like their photographs — wet, filthy, thin, frightened, or simply a different colour once muddy. Go and look yourself, repeatedly.

Several municipal systems publish lists of animals found recently, sometimes with a defined window of a couple of weeks, and hold periods vary by municipality. Ask directly how long an unclaimed animal is held where you live, and put the answer in your calendar rather than assuming somebody will call you.

Widen the radius for a dog. Do not widen it too fast for a cat.

## If you are the one who finds an animal

Contain it safely if you can do so without risk to yourself — a frightened animal may bite or scratch, and that is fear rather than character.

Get it scanned. A veterinary clinic, a municipal animal centre or a shelter will do it, usually without charge, and that single step resolves a large share of cases immediately. Given what the CVMA says about scanner variability, it is reasonable to have an animal scanned in more than one place if the first comes back empty.

Contact municipal animal services either way, because that is where the matching lost report will be. If you intend to keep looking for the owner yourself, post where you found it and when — but hold back one identifying detail so you can confirm a claim.

## Prevention, which is fifteen minutes

Almost everything that makes a recovery fast is done in advance.

- **A collar with a tag carrying a mobile number** — not a landline. This gets more animals home than every other method combined, because it needs no equipment and no institution.
- **A microchip whose registration is in your name, at your current address.** Implanting and registering are two separate steps, and the second is the one that gets missed — especially with an [adopted animal](/guides/adopting-a-pet-in-canada), where the record often still points at the rescue.
- **Ask for the chip to be scanned at the annual examination.**
- **Current photographs**, including a full-body shot showing markings, kept on your phone rather than only in the cloud.
- **A municipal licence** where your city issues one, since the tag is a second traceable number.
- **Know your own risk points.** Doors during a move, contractors, deliveries, fireworks, a torn screen. For cats, the [indoor and outdoor decision](/guides/indoor-or-outdoor-cats-in-canada) is largely a conversation about exactly this.
- **Update everything before you travel**, and add a second contact who will actually be reachable — the point the [travel guide](/guides/travelling-with-a-pet-in-canada) makes at length.

## The short version

Update the registry and file the municipal lost report before you do anything else. Then search like the species you have lost: outward and loudly for a dog, slowly and at ground level for a cat, because a frightened cat is hiding within a few houses and will not answer you.

Remember that a microchip only works when somebody scans it, that not all scanners read all chips, and that a collar tag with a mobile number needs no scanner at all.

Then go to the shelters in person, and go back.
