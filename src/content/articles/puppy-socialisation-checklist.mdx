Start puppy socialisation with short, comfortable introductions to the people, sounds, surfaces and everyday situations your dog will live with. Do not wait for a crowded outing or try to finish a list of encounters. Before the vaccination series is complete, agree suitable places and animal contacts with your veterinary team.

This checklist is for Canadian puppy households planning ordinary experiences, including cold-weather alternatives. It is not a behaviour diagnosis or a promise that early practice prevents every later fear. Sources and class-planning guidance were checked on October 26, 2026; this is an editorial review, not a veterinary review.

The [first-month guide](/guides/bringing-home-a-puppy-first-30-days) covers the wider home routine. Our [vaccination guide](/guides/puppy-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) explains why individual disease-risk advice belongs alongside socialisation planning.

## The window, and the number that matters

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior puts the primary and most important period at **the first three months of life** — so it is worth discussing a plan at the first veterinary visit rather than waiting until all vaccinations are finished.

[AVSAB's position statement](https://www.avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Puppy-Socialization-Position-Statement-FINAL.pdf) supports socialisation before full vaccination when experiences can be managed safely without overwhelming the puppy. It gives general puppy-class starting criteria of **7 to 8 weeks**, at least one vaccination set **seven days before** the first class, and a first deworming, with vaccinations kept current.

Those are not permission to use any venue. Your vet may advise different precautions for your puppy and local conditions, and providers set their own entry requirements. For example, [Winnipeg Humane Society's published class information](https://winnipeghumanesociety.ca/puppy-socialization-important-information/) specifies eight days after the first vaccination set and additional requirements. Check the actual class before booking; do not adjust vaccination dates yourself to fit a class.

## Read puppy comfort signals

Everything below depends on this, so it comes first.

Look for a generally **relaxed, willing** puppy. Its body is soft, it moves toward the thing on its own, it can take food, it looks back at you and then goes back to investigating. These observations help you judge whether to continue; they do not prove how the puppy feels.

Pause when the puppy becomes unusually still, frantic or tries to leave. The Merck Veterinary Manual lists low body posture, piloerection, vocalisation and displacement behaviours such as yawning or lip-licking among the signs of acute fear and anxiety. Other observations to notice include a tucked tail, ears back, turning the head away, freezing, or refusing food it would normally take.

Read them together rather than singly. No single observation reliably establishes the cause — a dog yawns because it is tired, licks its lips because it has eaten, and turns away because something else is more interesting. Merck classes yawning and lip-licking as *displacement* behaviours precisely because they show up in conflict and arousal generally, not in fear specifically. What makes them worth acting on is the combination, and the context: several at once, in a situation the puppy cannot leave, in a body that has otherwise gone still. [VCA lists refusal of treats among possible fear signs](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/puppy-behavior-and-training-training-basics), not as a stand-alone test. Taking food does not overrule freezing or attempts to escape. Stop a worrying interaction rather than using food to lure the puppy closer; a reduced appetite outside the situation also warrants a veterinary call.

Three rules follow:

**Let the puppy choose the distance.** Do not carry a puppy toward something and hold it there. Use a setting your vet considers suitable, with room to move away. The puppy need not approach or greet anyone. Retreat is information, not failure.

**Watch the recovery.** Notice whether the puppy returns to relaxed activity after a mild surprise. There is no universal ten-second or two-minute pass mark. If it remains worried, end the session and discuss persistent fear with your vet instead of repeating the exposure.

**End early.** Keep sessions brief, with breaks, and finish before the puppy is tired or uncomfortable. Duration is not a target to achieve.

Choose examples relevant to your household. Seeing something calmly at a distance can be enough for that session; direct contact is not a requirement.

## Where can a puppy socialise before vaccinations are complete?

Ask your vet about the specific location, not just whether the puppy can "go outside". Bring the vaccination record and describe who uses the space. A private yard used by unfamiliar dogs is different from a controlled home setting.

The [Ohio State veterinary socialisation and infection-prevention checklist](https://vet.osu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/socialization-and-id-prevention-checklist-final.pdf) considers health history, preventive care, hygiene and unknown dog traffic together. It recommends avoiding direct ground contact where dog traffic is unknown. Carrying or using an appropriate carrier or stroller may let a puppy observe, but does not make every setting risk-free.

Before choosing a class, ask:

- Which vaccination, parasite-prevention and health records are required, and when?
- How are surfaces cleaned, and what happens if a puppy is unwell?
- Can a puppy watch from a distance or leave instead of joining play?
- How are size, temperament and interaction style managed?
- Are rewards used without forced greetings or punishment?

For low-key home sessions, invite a calm visitor, introduce an ordinary object at a distance, or arrange contact with a known healthy dog only when your vet considers it suitable. Avoid using busy dog parks or communal dog-toileting areas as a default puppy classroom. If you do not yet have a clinic, our [Canadian veterinarian checklist](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada) helps you prepare the first call.

## The four ways this goes wrong

**Flooding.** Taking an unsure puppy closer to the thing it is worried about, or holding it there until it "gets used to it." [AAHA warns that forced social exposure can worsen fear](https://www.aaha.org/resources/2015-aaha-canine-and-feline-behavior-management-guidelines/age-and-behavior/). Stop rather than pressing on; seek veterinary guidance when a puppy withdraws or shows fear.

**Treating it as a numbers game.** A tally of meetings cannot tell you whether the puppy was comfortable. Record the response and what you would change, not just the number of encounters.

**Forced greetings.** Letting every stranger loom over and grab a puppy teaches it that people are unpredictable. It is entirely reasonable — and much better for the dog — to say "he's in training, could you crouch and let him come to you?" or simply "not today."

**Dog parks.** Unknown health histories and unpredictable play make a busy off-lead park a poor default for early introductions. Choose controlled contacts and suitable settings with your veterinary team.

## A winter puppy: adapt the setting, not the safety margin

Winter conditions differ across Canada and from day to day. Use comfortable indoor opportunities when cold, rain or slippery paths make an outing unsuitable: a visitor wearing a hat, an umbrella opened at a distance, or a quiet household sound. An observation session should be easy to end, not a reason to keep the puppy outside until a checklist is finished.

[CVMA cold-weather guidance](https://www.canadianveterinarians.net/related-resources/cold-weather-and-pets/) highlights cold discomfort, paw irritation from road salt, ice injuries and the fact that parked cars can become cold. Keep outdoor sessions brief, avoid hazardous surfaces and never leave a puppy in a vehicle as a socialisation exercise. Use our [winter care guide](/guides/winter-dog-care-in-canada) for the wider weather routine.

Early exposure does not guarantee that a dog will ignore a snowblower, fireworks or other noises for life. Keep watching the individual response as circumstances change.

## It does not stop at sixteen weeks

The first three months are a useful reason to start planning early, not a deadline after which learning stops. [AAHA's behaviour guidelines](https://www.aaha.org/resources/2015-aaha-canine-and-feline-behavior-management-guidelines/age-and-behavior/) emphasise individual variation and caution against restricting social experience to a particular period.

If a previously comfortable situation becomes difficult, do not insist that the dog "should know this by now." Stop the uncomfortable interaction and review what changed with your veterinary team. Development does not follow an identical calendar for every puppy.

What is not ordinary is fear that arrives suddenly, keeps getting worse, or comes with a dog that is off in itself — pain and illness can present as a behaviour change at any age. That is a call to your veterinary practice, and where it persists, to a qualified behaviour professional alongside them.

Continue suitable positive experiences as the dog grows. Review the plan when your home, routine or the dog's responses change.

## The short version

The early sensitive period often overlaps the vaccination series. Plan suitable experiences with your vet rather than waiting for every vaccination, and continue comfortable introductions as the dog grows.

Cover categories rather than counting encounters, and make sure surfaces, sounds and handling get as much attention as people and dogs do. Let the puppy choose the distance, watch how fast it recovers, and stop while it is still going well.

Record the setting, what the puppy did, and whether it could leave comfortably. Food interest is only one observation. Stop if the puppy is worried and get veterinary guidance rather than repeating frightening encounters.
