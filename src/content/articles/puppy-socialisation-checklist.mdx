Most people know they are supposed to socialise a puppy. Far fewer are told what that actually means, and the gap between the two produces a specific and common failure: an owner who diligently takes a puppy everywhere, exposes it to as much as possible as fast as possible, and ends up with a dog that is *more* fearful rather than less.

Socialisation is not exposure. It is exposure at an intensity the puppy can handle, in which nothing bad happens, repeated. The intensity part is the whole skill, and it is what this guide is about.

The [first-month guide](/guides/bringing-home-a-puppy-first-30-days) covers where socialisation sits in the schedule, and the [vaccination guide](/guides/puppy-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) covers why the window closes before the needles do. This one is the what and the how.

## The window, and the number that matters

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior puts the primary and most important period at **the first three months of life** — which means it is largely over by the time many owners feel ready to start.

AVSAB's position is unusually direct about the trade everyone worries about: behavioural problems, not infectious disease, are the leading cause of death in dogs under three years old, and they are the most common reason dogs are relinquished to shelters. On that basis it holds that puppies receiving socialisation before they are fully vaccinated should be **the standard of care** rather than a risk somebody takes.

Practically, AVSAB puts puppy classes as early as **7 to 8 weeks**, with a puppy having had at least one set of vaccines a minimum of **seven days before** the first class, plus a first deworming. A class that asks for none of that is telling you something about how it is run.

## The skill nobody teaches: reading the puppy

Everything below depends on this, so it comes first.

A puppy having a useful experience is **loose**. Its body is soft, it moves toward the thing on its own, it can take food, it looks back at you and then goes back to investigating. That puppy is learning that novel things are fine.

A puppy having a harmful experience is **still, or frantic**. Tucked tail, low body, ears back, lip-licking, yawning when not tired, turning its head away, freezing, or refusing food it would normally take. A puppy that will not eat is a puppy over threshold, and that is the single most reliable signal you have.

Three rules follow:

**Let the puppy choose the distance.** Do not carry it toward something and hold it there. Put it down at a distance where it is loose and let it approach on its own — or not. Retreat is information, not failure.

**Watch the recovery.** A puppy that startles at a bus and is back to sniffing in ten seconds has had a fine experience. One that startles and is still worried two minutes later was too close. Recovery time tells you more than the reaction did.

**End early.** Five good minutes beats twenty that finish badly, because the last thing that happens is what gets remembered.

## Where to do it while the series is unfinished

The tension is real, and the answer is not to wait. It is to choose settings where the infection risk is low:

- **Carry the puppy.** A bench outside a grocery store on a Saturday morning is an enormous amount of socialisation with no ground contact at all.
- **Your own yard and your own home**, with visitors coming to you.
- **Homes and yards of people whose dogs you know are healthy and vaccinated.**
- **A well-run puppy class** that requires proof of vaccination, keeps groups small, and disinfects between sessions.
- **The car**, parked somewhere with things happening, doors open, puppy on your lap, watching.

And places worth waiting on: dog parks, pet shop floors, highway rest stops, and any communal grass where large numbers of unknown dogs pass through.

## The four ways this goes wrong

**Flooding.** Taking an unsure puppy closer to the thing it is worried about, or holding it there until it "gets used to it." It does not get used to it. It learns it cannot escape, which is a worse lesson than the one you were trying to avoid.

**Treating it as a numbers game.** A hundred hurried introductions produce a dog that finds novelty overwhelming. Twenty calm ones produce a dog that finds novelty unremarkable.

**Forced greetings.** Letting every stranger loom over and grab a puppy teaches it that people are unpredictable. It is entirely reasonable — and much better for the dog — to say "he's in training, could you crouch and let him come to you?" or simply "not today."

**Dog parks.** An off-lead space full of unknown adult dogs of unknown temperament is not socialisation. One bad experience at this age can outweigh weeks of good ones.

## The winter puppy

A puppy that arrives in November has a genuinely harder socialisation period than one that arrives in May, and almost nothing written about this accounts for it.

Fewer people are outdoors. Nobody lingers. Patios are shut, children are inside, and the puppy's whole social world is your household for three months. Meanwhile it is meeting an entire category — snow, ice, salt, boots, shovels, snowblowers, people muffled to the eyes in hoods and scarves — that a summer puppy will not encounter until it is much older and less flexible.

Compensate deliberately: invite people to you rather than waiting to meet them out; use the car as a mobile viewing platform; do more sound work indoors, because you have the time; and treat the winter-specific items as the opportunity they are. A dog that met a snowblower calmly at ten weeks is a dog that ignores it for life. The [winter care guide](/guides/winter-dog-care-in-canada) covers keeping the outings short enough that they stay pleasant.

## It does not stop at sixteen weeks

The primary window closes, and the work does not. Many dogs go through a second period of increased wariness during adolescence, when things they previously accepted suddenly become suspicious.

This is normal, it is not a sign that the early work failed, and the response is the same as before: more distance, lower intensity, good things happening, no forcing. What fails is deciding the dog "should know this by now."

Keep a low-key version running for the first two years. It costs a few minutes a week.

<Checklist title="A workable weekly rhythm">

- One new environment, at a distance the puppy stays loose in
- Two or three new people, crouching and letting the puppy approach
- One new surface underfoot
- Sound work indoors, quiet enough that the puppy keeps eating
- Two minutes of handling practice a day, every day
- One car journey that ends somewhere pleasant
- Short alone-time practice, daily, in seconds then minutes
- Nothing at all on the days the puppy is overtired — sleep is part of the plan

</Checklist>

## The short version

The window is the first three months and it closes before the vaccination series does, so socialise early, in low-risk settings, deliberately.

Cover categories rather than counting encounters, and make sure surfaces, sounds and handling get as much attention as people and dogs do. Let the puppy choose the distance, watch how fast it recovers, and stop while it is still going well.

A puppy that eats treats in a new place is learning. A puppy that will not is over threshold, and the answer is always more distance rather than more exposure.
