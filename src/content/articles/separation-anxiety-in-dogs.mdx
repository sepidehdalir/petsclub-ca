There is a distinction that decides everything about what you should do next, and almost every article about this subject blurs it.

A bored dog left alone chews something and then goes to sleep. A distressed dog does not settle at all. The first is a management problem you can solve with a better chew and more exercise. The second is a welfare problem with a recognised treatment protocol, and the things people try first — more exercise, a longer walk, a firmer tone, leaving the radio on — do not touch it.

This is about the second one: how to tell, what actually works, and why the usual approach fails.

## Protest fades. Distress escalates.

The most reliable signal is the shape of the noise over time, not its volume.

A dog complaining about being shut out of the room grumbles, gives it a minute or two, and settles. A dog in genuine distress starts and does not stop, or gets worse the longer you are gone. That difference is visible on any phone camera left running, and **filming a departure is the single most useful thing you can do before doing anything else.** Most owners are guessing about what happens after they leave. You do not have to.

What distress commonly looks like:

- Vocalisation that begins within minutes of departure and continues, rather than tapering
- Destruction concentrated at exit points — doors, door frames, window sills — rather than distributed around the house
- House-soiling in a dog that is otherwise reliably clean
- Drooling, panting or pacing well beyond what the temperature explains
- Attempts to escape confinement, sometimes to the point of injury
- Frantic, prolonged greeting rather than ordinary pleasure at your return
- An animal that shadows you from room to room and becomes agitated during your departure routine, before you have gone anywhere

What it is not: revenge, guilt, or a dog "knowing it did wrong." The appeasing posture people read as guilt is a response to your arrival and your body language, not evidence of understanding.

<VetNote>
Rule out medical causes before treating this as behavioural. House-soiling
can have urinary, gastrointestinal or endocrine causes; restlessness and
vocalisation can reflect pain, cognitive decline in an older dog, or several
treatable conditions. Sudden onset in an adult dog that previously coped is a
particular reason to book an appointment rather than start a training plan.
And a dog that injures itself trying to escape — damaged nails, bleeding gums,

does not diagnose or treat anything.
</VetNote>

## The protocol that actually has evidence behind it

The treatment described in veterinary behaviour references is **graduated departure training**, and its logic is narrow and specific: build the dog's tolerance for being alone in increments small enough that it never becomes anxious, because an episode of anxiety undoes progress rather than building it.

Two elements do the work.

**Simulated departures of gradually increasing length**, performed as daily sessions rather than as real absences. You are not leaving the house because you need to go somewhere. You are leaving because the exercise is the point, and you are coming back before the dog has any reason to worry.

**A safety signal.** This is a novel object or sound the dog has never associated with a previous departure — a particular mat, a specific radio station, a lamp. You present it only for departures the dog is going to cope with, and remove it for the ones it will not. Over time the signal itself becomes information: *this is the kind of leaving where you are fine.* It makes departures predictable in a way that a dog can actually use, which is precisely what an unpredictable schedule denies it.

The crucial constraint, stated plainly in the veterinary literature: **moving too quickly provokes anxiety and sabotages the whole programme.** If you go from three minutes to thirty because three minutes went well, you have not saved time. You have gone backwards.

## Why most plans fail, and it is not the dog

There is a finding worth knowing before you start. In work evaluating treatments for separation anxiety, dogs whose owners were given **more than five instructions did worse** than dogs whose owners were given fewer.

That is not a comment on owners. It is a comment on plans. A protocol with fourteen components is a protocol nobody completes, and partial compliance on a graduated programme is close to worthless because the increments are the mechanism.

So: pick a small number of things and do them properly. If a trainer hands you a page of instructions, ask which three matter most this fortnight.

## What to stop doing

- **Punishment, in any form.** It adds fear to a fear problem, and the dog cannot connect it to something that happened an hour ago in your absence.
- **Longer walks as the treatment.** Exercise is good for a dog and does not address distress at being alone. A tired anxious dog is an anxious dog.
- **Getting another dog to keep it company.** Sometimes this helps. Frequently it produces two dogs with a problem, and it is an expensive experiment to run on a hunch.
- **Flooding** — leaving the dog for progressively longer to "get used to it." This is the opposite of the protocol and it reliably makes things worse.
- **Fighting a crate.** Crate work and alone-time training are the same project, and a crate suits many dogs well — the [crate training guide](/guides/crate-training-a-puppy-in-canada) covers building that association properly. But a dog that panics in confinement needs a pen or a gated room instead. Being right about the tool matters far less than the dog being safe.

## Medication, framed honestly

For moderate to severe cases, veterinary behaviour references describe medication as something that may be needed **to augment** behaviour modification — not to replace it. The purpose is to bring arousal down far enough that the dog can actually learn from the training, which a dog in a panic cannot.

This is a veterinary decision, it involves prescription drugs with real effects, and nothing you can buy over the counter is a substitute. What you can usefully do is raise it as a question rather than waiting to be offered it: *is this dog distressed enough that medication would make the training work better?*

## Prevention, for anyone reading this early

If you have a puppy and no problem yet, this is the cheapest section on the site.

Teach being alone from the first full day, in seconds rather than minutes, long before you need to leave for real. It is the most-skipped step in the [first month](/guides/bringing-home-a-puppy-first-30-days) and by a distance the hardest to retrofit. A puppy that has never been alone until the day everyone returns to work learns that solitude is an emergency.

## The Canadian part, which is about housing

In an apartment or a condo this stops being only a welfare problem and becomes a tenancy one, because the mechanism by which people lose pet-friendly housing is a noise complaint.

Where that leaves you depends on the province. In Ontario a no-pets clause in a lease is void — but a landlord can still act on the basis of a particular animal's behaviour, so the complaint is the exposure rather than the clause. In British Columbia a tenancy agreement may prohibit pets outright and breaching it can end the tenancy. In Quebec a lessor may refuse animals and a signed clause binds. The [renting guide](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada) sets out all three.

Two practical consequences. Talk to your neighbours early rather than after a complaint — a neighbour who knows you are working on it behaves differently from one who thinks you are ignoring it. And if you are in a condominium, remember there are two rulebooks and the stricter one wins.

<Checklist title="A workable first fortnight">

- Film a departure before changing anything, and watch what actually happens
- Book a veterinary appointment to rule out medical causes, especially if this is new
- Establish the dog's current threshold honestly — the point at which it is still calm, even if that is thirty seconds
- Choose one safety signal and use it only for departures the dog will cope with
- Run short simulated departures daily, well inside the threshold
- Make your departure routine boring by repeating it without leaving
- Keep arrivals and departures undramatic at both ends
- Arrange cover so the dog is not left beyond its threshold while you train — a sitter, daycare, a neighbour
- Ask your clinic whether medication would help the training work
- Pick no more than three things to do properly this fortnight

</Checklist>

## When to bring in a professional

If the dog is injuring itself, if the distress is severe, if there has been no progress in a few weeks of consistent work, or if you are running out of the capacity to keep this up — that is the point, and it is not a failure.

Start with your own veterinary clinic. They can exclude medical contributors, discuss medication, and refer you onward. Veterinarians in Canada are licensed provincially, and the guide to [choosing a practice](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada) names the regulator in each province if you want to check anyone's standing.

## The short version

Work out whether it is protest or distress, because they need different things and a camera will tell you in one afternoon. Rule out medical causes first. Then build tolerance in increments small enough that the dog never rehearses panic, using a safety signal so departures become predictable.

Go slower than feels reasonable. Do three things properly rather than twelve badly. And treat the housing risk as real, because in most of urban Canada it is the noise, not the chewing, that ends a tenancy.
