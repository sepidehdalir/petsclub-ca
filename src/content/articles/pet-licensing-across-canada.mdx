There is no single Canada-wide pet-licence system. City or town offices handle most dog and cat registration, but Quebec also has a province-wide rule requiring dog owners to register with their municipality. Check the official rules where you live, including the species covered, starting age, renewal date, fees and identification requirements. A licence, a microchip registration and any vaccination requirement are separate checks.

The cities differ in species covered, application rules, starting ages and the benefits associated with registration. Calgary and Vancouver specify a three-month threshold, while Edmonton requires licensing for cats and dogs over six months. Ottawa's bylaw requires cat and dog registration, but its three-month **rabies-vaccination** requirement should not be treated as a documented licence-registration age threshold.

## Quebec's province-wide dog-registration rule

Quebec is an important exception to the shorthand that pet licensing is purely municipal. The province requires a dog owner or keeper to **register the dog annually with their municipality** and keep the issued identification tag on the dog. Municipalities administer registration and may impose stricter local rules. Read the [Quebec government's dog-control guidance](https://www.quebec.ca/agriculture-environnement-et-ressources-naturelles/sante-animale/animaux-compagnie/encadrement-chiens) and [official identification guidance](https://www.quebec.ca/agriculture-environnement-et-ressources-naturelles/sante-animale/animaux-compagnie/identification). Whether a **cat** must be registered still depends on the municipality. Check the official local site for fees and the application process. The existence of a provincial dog rule does not create a Canada-wide registry.

## The four things that vary

**Which species.** The assumption that licensing is a dog thing is the most common and the most expensive mistake, because it is usually made by cat owners who never find out otherwise. Toronto, Ottawa, Calgary and Edmonton all license cats. Plenty of municipalities do not. Yours is a question with a definite answer on one specific web page.

**Whether indoor animals are exempt.** Calgary and Edmonton include indoor cats in their licensing rules. Check your own municipality rather than assuming that indoor status creates an exemption.

**The age threshold.** Calgary and Vancouver specify three months for the relevant dog or cat licensing rules, while Edmonton requires licences for cats and dogs over six months. Ottawa requires registration, but the three-month threshold verified in its bylaw concerns rabies vaccination; do not present it as a registration starting age. Check the specific rule for a young animal rather than assuming every city uses the same threshold.

**What is bundled.** Do not assume that paying a licence fee means a microchip has already been implanted or its registry updated. Edmonton's [Don't Skip the Chip program](https://www.edmonton.ca/residential_neighbourhoods/pets_wildlife/pet-microchip-program) says its pet licence includes a microchip, but participation requires an appointment, proof of ownership and a valid City licence. Confirm arrangements before attending. A collar tag, an implanted microchip and the contact records attached to each remain distinct.

## Before you apply or renew

Use the municipality's own application page rather than a third-party payment link. Prepare the information it requests and check the total before submitting.

<Checklist title="Pet-licence application checklist">

- Confirm the municipality serving your home address, the species covered and the minimum licensing age
- Check the current fee category, renewal period and any documented eligibility for a reduced rate
- Have your pet's approximate birth date, identifying description and current contact information ready
- If relevant, find the spay/neuter date and clinic, plus the existing microchip or tattoo number
- Check whether the city needs supporting records, and use only its official submission route
- Save the confirmation, record the renewal date and check how to replace a missing tag
- When you move or change your phone number, update municipal records and the microchip registry separately

</Checklist>

Edmonton's [current licensing page](https://www.edmonton.ca/residential_neighbourhoods/pets_wildlife/pet-licences-for-residents) specifically asks applicants to know their pet's birth date or approximation, spay/neuter details where available and microchip or tattoo number where applicable. Other municipalities may ask for different evidence. Keep documents private; this website does not collect licence applications or payments.

## Why it is worth doing beyond avoiding a fine

The enforcement argument is real — these are ticketable offences and the fines are not trivial — but it is the least interesting reason.

A licence tag is a **visible identifier that requires no equipment to read.** A microchip needs somebody to catch the animal, take it somewhere with a scanner, and have that scanner detect that chip — and the Canadian Veterinary Medical Association is explicit that not all scanners read all chips, because several RFID frequencies are in use. A tag on a collar needs a neighbour with a phone.

That is why the [guide to a missing pet](/guides/when-a-pet-goes-missing-in-canada) treats the licence tag as one of three independent layers of identification rather than as paperwork. The layers exist because each of them fails differently.

Municipal licensing also means the city holds a record connecting a description to a household, which is what makes a lost report matchable at the other end.

## The spay and neuter differential

Where a municipality offers a lower fee for an altered animal — and it is common — the saving is annual and recurs for the animal's whole life.

**Toronto publishes its own figures, and they show the scale of the spay/neuter differential.** As listed on the City of Toronto pet licensing fee page in September 2026, an annual licence costs $25.00 for a spayed or neutered dog against $60.00 for one that is not, and $15.00 against $50.00 for cats. Owners aged 65 and over get half off, and households with income under $50,000 may have fees subsidised or waived. These are Toronto’s numbers on that date, not a Canadian price: every city sets its own, and Toronto resets these periodically.

Those are Toronto's fees and nobody else's — every municipality sets its own, and this is an illustration of how large the differential can be rather than a figure to budget against anywhere else. But run the unaltered dog rate out over a fifteen-year life and the gap is not a token. It is one of two Canadian costs that the [spaying and neutering guide](/guides/spaying-and-neutering-in-canada) argues most owners never put into the calculation.

Worth noting what this is not: an argument for the timing of the procedure. When to spay or neuter is a veterinary decision that now depends heavily on projected adult size, and a licence discount is not a reason to do it earlier than your veterinarian recommends.

## Licensing is not the rabies rule, except where it is

These two get conflated constantly. Registration and rabies immunisation are separate legal requirements; their administering levels and details depend on jurisdiction.

Rabies vaccination requirements are **provincial**. Ontario requires cats, dogs and ferrets “three months of age or over” to be immunized (R.R.O. 1990, Reg. 567, s. 1); British Columbia recommends vaccination, and we do not assert a province-wide legal requirement there. That divergence is set out in the [puppy](/guides/puppy-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) and [kitten](/guides/kitten-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) vaccination guides.

Registration is generally administered **municipally** — with Quebec's province-wide dog-registration requirement as one important legal layer. A municipality can also specify vaccination requirements. Ottawa's animal bylaw includes a three-month rabies-immunisation requirement, but that age is **not evidence of the city's earliest registration age**. Check registration and vaccination separately.

The practical consequence: satisfying one does not automatically satisfy the other, and the vaccination certificate is often what the licence application asks for.

## What a licence does not do

Four things it is routinely assumed to cover and does not.

**It is not a substitute for a microchip.** They are different layers that fail in different ways — a tag can come off a collar, and a chip needs somebody with a working scanner. Keep both, and keep the chip's registration current, which is a separate action from renewing a licence.

**It is not permission from your landlord or your condominium.** A city licence says the municipality knows the animal exists. It says nothing about whether you are contractually allowed to keep it where you live, which is a provincial tenancy question and a condominium declaration question, and the stricter of the two wins. The [renting guide](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada) sets out how differently that lands in Ontario, British Columbia and Quebec.

**Do not assume it travels.** Licensing is municipal, so the city you move to runs its own system with its own requirements, fees and renewal dates. We have not checked whether any Canadian municipality will recognise another’s licence, and we are not claiming none does — the practical point is that you check the new municipality rather than assume, including across a boundary you might not think of as a move.

**It is not proof of vaccination**, except where a municipality has separately made vaccination a condition — as Ottawa has. Two obligations, sometimes satisfied with the same certificate, but not the same obligation.

## When you move

This is the step that gets missed in a move, because it is nobody's job to tell you.

<Checklist title="Moving to a new municipality">

- Check the new city's animal bylaw before you assume the rules travel with you — species covered, age threshold, and whether indoor cats are included
- Check licensing in the new municipality rather than assuming an existing licence carries over
- Update the microchip registry with the new address and phone number, which is a separate action from licensing
- Update the tag on the collar if it carries an address
- Check any limit on the number of animals per household, which some municipalities set
- Check leash, at-large and off-leash-area rules, which are also municipal and also differ
- If you are renting, confirm the pet position under your new province's tenancy law — it is [not the same in every province](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada)

</Checklist>

Moving between provinces changes two layers at once — the municipal rules and, potentially, the provincial rabies position — which is worth an hour before the move rather than a discovery afterwards. [The full sequence is here](/guides/moving-provinces-with-a-pet).

## Where to actually look

For municipal fees, local application steps and tag renewal, use your own municipality's official animal bylaw or licensing page rather than a third-party payment link. In Quebec, consult the provincial dog-registration rules as well. This guide illustrates five cities; it cannot replace local regulations or cover every municipality.

Search your city's name with "animal bylaw" or "pet licence". If you are outside a city, the county, regional municipality or district will have the equivalent, and rural municipalities frequently have quite different rules again.

## The short version

There is no Canada-wide pet-licence system. Toronto, Ottawa, Calgary and Edmonton license cats as well as dogs; Calgary and Vancouver specify three months for relevant licences, while Edmonton's threshold is over six months. Do not confuse Ottawa's three-month **rabies** rule with a pet-registration age. Quebec also requires annual dog registration under province-wide rules administered by municipalities. Edmonton's microchip programme requires an appointment, and Calgary and Edmonton include indoor cats.

Do not conflate it with the rabies requirement, which is provincial — although Ottawa layers its own on top, so both apply.

And treat the tag as identification rather than bureaucracy. It is the one layer that works without a scanner, a clinic, or anybody knowing what a microchip is.
