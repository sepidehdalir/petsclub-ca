Almost every other rule about owning an animal in this country is provincial. Tenancy is provincial. Rabies vaccination is provincial. Who is allowed to call themselves a veterinarian is provincial.

Licensing is not. **Licensing is municipal**, which means it is decided by your city or town and by nobody else — and the differences between two Canadian cities are larger than most owners realise. Whether your cat needs a licence at all, at what age, what the licence includes, and what it costs are four separate questions with four locally-set answers.

This is what actually varies, and why the answer your friend gave you is probably right about their city and wrong about yours.

Read down that table and the pattern is not that some cities are stricter. It is that the *shape* of the rule differs: the species covered, the age at which the obligation starts — three months in three of these cities and six in another — and what you get in return.

## The four things that vary

**Which species.** The assumption that licensing is a dog thing is the most common and the most expensive mistake, because it is usually made by cat owners who never find out otherwise. Toronto, Ottawa, Calgary and Edmonton all license cats. Plenty of municipalities do not. Yours is a question with a definite answer on one specific web page.

**Whether indoor animals are exempt.** Generally, no. Calgary and Edmonton both explicitly include indoor cats, and the logic is straightforward once you see it — the licence exists to get a lost animal home, and an indoor cat that gets out is precisely the animal least equipped to find its own way back.

**The age threshold.** Three months in Calgary, Ottawa and Vancouver; six months in Edmonton. This matters most for anyone who has just acquired a young animal and assumes there is a grace period measured in years.

**What is bundled.** Edmonton's licence includes a microchip, which is unusual and genuinely valuable. Several cities issue a numbered tag that functions as a second traceable identifier alongside a chip. And reduced fees for spayed or neutered animals are common enough to be worth checking before you assume the sticker price.

## Why it is worth doing beyond avoiding a fine

The enforcement argument is real — these are ticketable offences and the fines are not trivial — but it is the least interesting reason.

A licence tag is a **visible identifier that requires no equipment to read.** A microchip needs somebody to catch the animal, take it somewhere with a scanner, and have that scanner detect that chip — and the Canadian Veterinary Medical Association is explicit that not all scanners read all chips, because several RFID frequencies are in use. A tag on a collar needs a neighbour with a phone.

That is why the [guide to a missing pet](/guides/when-a-pet-goes-missing-in-canada) treats the licence tag as one of three independent layers of identification rather than as paperwork. The layers exist because each of them fails differently.

Municipal licensing also means the city holds a record connecting a description to a household, which is what makes a lost report matchable at the other end.

## The spay and neuter differential

Where a municipality offers a lower fee for an altered animal — and it is common — the saving is annual and recurs for the animal's whole life. Over fifteen years that is a real number rather than a token, and it is one of two Canadian costs that the [spaying and neutering guide](/guides/spaying-and-neutering-in-canada) argues most owners never put into the calculation.

Worth noting what this is not: an argument for the timing of the procedure. When to spay or neuter is a veterinary decision that now depends heavily on projected adult size, and a licence discount is not a reason to do it earlier than your veterinarian recommends.

## Licensing is not the rabies rule, except where it is

These two get conflated constantly, and they are separate obligations from separate levels of government.

Rabies vaccination requirements are **provincial**. Ontario requires dogs, cats and ferrets over three months to be vaccinated; British Columbia recommends it but does not compel it by law. That divergence is set out in the [puppy](/guides/puppy-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) and [kitten](/guides/kitten-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) vaccination guides.

Licensing is **municipal**, and separate — but a municipality can attach its own requirement on top. Ottawa's animal bylaw independently requires dogs and cats three months and over to be currently immunised against rabies, which means an Ottawa owner is meeting a provincial obligation and a municipal one at the same time, from the same appointment.

The practical consequence: satisfying one does not automatically satisfy the other, and the vaccination certificate is often what the licence application asks for.

## What a licence does not do

Four things it is routinely assumed to cover and does not.

**It is not a substitute for a microchip.** They are different layers that fail in different ways — a tag can come off a collar, and a chip needs somebody with a working scanner. Keep both, and keep the chip's registration current, which is a separate action from renewing a licence.

**It is not permission from your landlord or your condominium.** A city licence says the municipality knows the animal exists. It says nothing about whether you are contractually allowed to keep it where you live, which is a provincial tenancy question and a condominium declaration question, and the stricter of the two wins. The [renting guide](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada) sets out how differently that lands in Ontario, British Columbia and Quebec.

**It does not travel.** Licences are issued by one municipality and generally do not transfer to another, including across a city boundary you might not think of as a move.

**It is not proof of vaccination**, except where a municipality has separately made vaccination a condition — as Ottawa has. Two obligations, sometimes satisfied with the same certificate, but not the same obligation.

## When you move

This is the step that gets missed in a move, because it is nobody's job to tell you.

<Checklist title="Moving to a new municipality">

- Check the new city's animal bylaw before you assume the rules travel with you — species covered, age threshold, and whether indoor cats are included
- Licence in the new municipality, and do not assume an existing licence transfers, because generally it does not
- Update the microchip registry with the new address and phone number, which is a separate action from licensing
- Update the tag on the collar if it carries an address
- Check any limit on the number of animals per household, which some municipalities set
- Check leash, at-large and off-leash-area rules, which are also municipal and also differ
- If you are renting, confirm the pet position under your new province's tenancy law — it is [not the same in every province](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada)

</Checklist>

Moving between provinces changes two layers at once — the municipal rules and, potentially, the provincial rabies position — which is worth an hour before the move rather than a discovery afterwards. [The full sequence is here](/guides/moving-provinces-with-a-pet).

## Where to actually look

The authoritative source is your own municipality's animal bylaw or licensing page, and nothing else. Not a forum, not a national article, and not this one — what is written here about five cities is accurate to those five cities and tells you only that the answer varies.

Search your city's name with "animal bylaw" or "pet licence". If you are outside a city, the county, regional municipality or district will have the equivalent, and rural municipalities frequently have quite different rules again.

## The short version

Licensing is municipal and the variation is real: Toronto, Ottawa, Calgary and Edmonton license cats as well as dogs, Edmonton's threshold is six months where others use three, Edmonton bundles a microchip, and indoor cats are generally not exempt.

Do not conflate it with the rabies requirement, which is provincial — although Ottawa layers its own on top, so both apply.

And treat the tag as identification rather than bureaucracy. It is the one layer that works without a scanner, a clinic, or anybody knowing what a microchip is.
