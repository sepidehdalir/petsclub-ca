A cat that has stopped using its litter box is not making a point. It is not angry about the new sofa, it is not punishing you for the weekend away, and it does not understand the concept of spite.

What it is doing is telling you that something has changed — in its body, in its box, or in its world — and the entire job is working out which of those three it is. Getting that order right matters more than anything else in this article, because the second and third are wasted effort if it is the first.

## Rule out the body first. Always.

This is not a hedge. It is the first step in the veterinary guidelines on the subject, and it is first for a reason: **any condition that affects how much a cat urinates, how often, how urgently, how comfortably, or whether it can physically get to the box can produce exactly the behaviour you are seeing.**

The AAFP and ISFM house-soiling guidelines — endorsed by AAHA — put excluding medical problems ahead of every behavioural intervention. The conditions that commonly present this way include inflammation of the urinary tract, which makes urinating painful and increases frequency and urgency; feline idiopathic cystitis, which is bladder pain with a stress component; and systemic conditions such as hyperthyroidism and diabetes that change how much a cat drinks and passes.

And there is one that is not merely a cause of house-soiling but an emergency in its own right. A cat straining in the box and producing little or nothing may be **obstructed**, and that is covered properly in the [guide to urinary blockage and FLUTD](/guides/cat-urinary-blockage-flutd). Do not work through this article first if that is what you are seeing.

There is also a straightforward physical cause that gets missed constantly in older cats: it hurts to get into the box. An arthritic cat facing high sides, or a flight of stairs between it and the only tray in the house, will eventually choose the floor — and that is a [mobility problem](/guides/arthritis-and-mobility-in-dogs-and-cats) wearing a behaviour problem's clothes.

## Then work out which of two things it is

Once medical causes are excluded, house-soiling divides into categories that look similar on the carpet and need completely different responses.

**Toileting outside the box.** The cat squats, produces a normal volume, and does it on a horizontal surface — often near the box, or on something soft. This is usually a statement about the box: where it is, what is in it, how many there are, or what happened the last time the cat used it.

**Marking.** Usually urine, usually sprayed backwards onto a vertical surface with the tail upright and quivering, usually a small volume, and usually in socially significant places — doorways, windows, the route another cat takes. This is communication, not toileting, and adding a nicer litter box will not touch it. Marking is driven by something the cat perceives as a threat to territory: another cat in the house, a cat visible through a window, a new person, a renovation, a move.

Telling them apart is the difference between spending three weeks fixing the wrong thing.

Beyond the box itself, the guidelines pair box optimisation with meeting a cat's broader environmental needs — somewhere to hide, somewhere high, resources it does not have to compete for, and an outlet for normal behaviour. That is the subject of the [enrichment guide](/guides/indoor-cat-enrichment-canadian-homes) rather than this one, but the two problems are frequently the same problem.

## Cleaning up, which affects whether it happens again

An area that still smells of urine to a cat is an invitation, and household cleaners do not remove it — several make it worse.

**Use an enzymatic cleaner** made for pet urine, saturate rather than wipe, and let it work for as long as the label says rather than blotting it straight off. **Never use anything ammonia-based**: ammonia is a component of urine, and to a cat it reads as a spot that has already been used.

Do not punish. Not shouting, not the spray bottle, not carrying the cat to the box and putting it in. Punishment does not connect to a decision the cat made twenty minutes ago; what it reliably teaches is that you are unpredictable, which adds stress to a problem stress is frequently causing.

## When it is marking

Different problem, different fix. The box audit will not solve it, and neither will more boxes — though both are still worth having right.

The work is finding and reducing the trigger:

- **Another cat in the house** is the most common one, and it is often not an obvious fight. A cat that blocks a doorway, or stares from across the room, is applying pressure that the marking cat is responding to. The [second-cat guide](/guides/introducing-a-second-cat) covers separating resources, adding vertical escape routes, and what a properly staged introduction looks like — including going back a stage, which is a legitimate move at any point.
- **Cats outside**, seen through a window, are an enormously common trigger and one owners overlook because the intruder never comes in. Block the sightline at cat height, or discourage the visitor from the garden.
- **Change** — a move, a renovation, a new baby, a new partner, a different work pattern, furniture rearranged.

Synthetic pheromone products are sometimes used alongside environmental changes. Treat them as a possible adjunct rather than the intervention.

Neutering reduces marking in many intact cats, and it is not a fix on its own once the behaviour is established.

<Checklist title="A workable sequence">

- Veterinary appointment first, before changing anything — and urgently if the cat is straining and producing little
- Establish which it is: normal-volume squatting on horizontal surfaces, or small-volume spraying on vertical ones
- Count the boxes: one per cat plus one, in separate rooms
- Uncover them, check the sides are low enough, and go up a size
- Switch to fine-grained unscented litter, transitioning gradually
- Scoop daily and stop using scented cleaners on the box
- Clean every soiled area with an enzymatic cleaner, never ammonia
- If it is marking, look for the trigger — another cat, a cat outside, a change in the house
- Give it weeks rather than days, and change one variable at a time so you learn something
- Go back to the clinic if there is no progress, or if anything about the cat changes

</Checklist>

## Why this is worth the effort

Litter box problems are among the most common reasons cats end up surrendered, and they are also among the most fixable — which is a bleak combination. A large share of cases are a box in the wrong place, a cover the cat dislikes, a second cat guarding a doorway, or a condition a veterinarian can treat.

The failure mode is almost never a lack of effort. It is doing the behavioural work first, getting nowhere, and concluding the cat is the problem — when the answer was in the part that got skipped.

## The short version

Rule out medical causes first, and treat straining with little or no urine as an emergency rather than a behaviour problem.

Then decide whether you are looking at toileting or marking, because they need opposite responses. For toileting, audit the boxes: one per cat plus one, in separate quiet rooms, big, uncovered, low-sided, fine unscented litter, scooped daily. For marking, find what the cat is responding to — usually another cat, and often one it can see but you have stopped noticing.

Clean with enzymes, never ammonia. Never punish. And give any change several weeks before deciding it did not work.
