Most trips with an animal go fine. The ones that do not usually fail on something ordinary — a booking that turned out to allow one cat and not two dogs, a microchip registered to a phone number from two addresses ago, a bag of food that ran out in a town with no pet shop.

This is a planning guide for travelling inside Canada: what to settle before you book, how to make a long drive tolerable for everyone in the car, and what to have arranged at the other end. Crossing an international border is a different problem with its own documentation and timelines, and it is out of scope here.

## Start with a fair question

Not every animal should travel, and “should we bring them?” is a real question rather than a formality.

Travel suits an animal that is broadly healthy, reasonably adaptable, and does not fall apart in unfamiliar places. It suits a lot of dogs. It suits far fewer cats — most cats are attached to territory rather than to scenery, and a week in a strange room is often harder on them than a week at home with a competent sitter. Very young, very old, unwell, recovering or highly anxious animals are all cases where staying home is frequently the kinder answer.

If the trip is short and the alternative is decent, the alternative is often better. If the trip is a move, or a fortnight, or the animal is a dog who is happier in the car than out of it, plan properly and go.

## Identification: the thing that actually matters

If an animal is going to be lost, it will be somewhere unfamiliar, where it does not know its way back and you do not know the streets. Identification is the whole recovery plan.

- **Update the microchip registry before you leave.** A chip is only a number; the number is useless if the record attached to it lists a phone you no longer use. Log in, check the phone and email, and add a second contact who will be reachable while you are away. This is the single most valuable ten minutes of the entire preparation.
- **Confirm the chip is actually readable.** A clinic can scan it in seconds at a routine appointment.
- **Put a visible tag on the collar** with a mobile number, not a landline at home. If you are staying somewhere for a while, a temporary tag with the local address is worth the few dollars.
- **Take current photographs** — a clear head shot and a full-body one, showing any distinctive markings. Keep them on your phone, not only in the cloud.
- **Check the fit of the collar or harness.** Escapes happen at rest stops, at the moment of transfer between car and building, and in unfamiliar hallways — all situations where a frightened animal backs out of loose equipment. A properly fitted harness on a nervous dog is worth more than any amount of vigilance.

## Car travel

Driving is how most Canadians travel with an animal, and it is the mode you have the most control over.

**Restraint is not optional.** A loose animal in a car is a distraction, is unprotected in a collision, and becomes a hazard to everyone if a door is opened at the roadside afterwards. The workable options are a secured crate, a carrier belted in place, or a crash-tested harness attached to a seatbelt. Whatever you use, secure it so it cannot slide or tip. Cats travel in a carrier, always — a cat loose in a car footwell during a hard stop is a genuinely dangerous situation.

**Never leave an animal alone in a parked car.** In summer a vehicle heats far faster than people expect, and shade and a cracked window do not solve it. In a Canadian winter the same car becomes a cold box within minutes. Plan the trip so that someone can stay with the animal, or so that stops happen where it can come with you.

**Build up to it if the animal is not used to cars.** Short trips that end somewhere good, before the week you actually need the animal to sit in a car for six hours.

**Motion sickness and travel anxiety are treatable**, and both are worth raising at a routine appointment well before departure rather than the night before. Do not give any human medication, and do not repurpose a prescription written for another animal.

**Feeding on the road.** A lighter meal several hours before departure suits most animals better than a full one immediately before. Keep water available at stops rather than sloshing in a bowl for the whole drive.

### The rhythm of a long drive

- **Stop every couple of hours** for a leashed toilet break, water and a short walk. This is as much for the driver as the dog.
- **Leash before the door opens.** Every time. Highway rest stops are the classic place for a bolt.
- **Never open a cat carrier in a moving or unsecured vehicle**, including at a rest stop with the doors open.
- **Keep the routine recognisable.** Meals at roughly the usual times, the usual food, the usual bed or blanket in the car. Familiar smells do a lot of the settling.
- **Watch the temperature in the back.** Sun through a rear window, or a cargo area that the heating does not really reach, are both easy to miss from the front seat.
- **For cats on a multi-day drive**, a covered litter tray set up in the room each night, and the carrier left open as the safe place. Do not plan to let a cat out in the car.

## Lodging

**Confirm the pet policy in writing at the time of booking, and keep the confirmation.** “Pet friendly” is a marketing phrase, not a specification, and it varies enormously.

Ask, and get the answer in an email:

- How many animals, of what species, and is there a size or weight limit?
- What is the fee, is it per night or per stay, and is it refundable?
- Which rooms are pet rooms, and where are they in the building?
- **May the animal be left alone in the room?** Many places say no, and this single answer can reshape an entire day’s plan.
- Are animals allowed in common areas, on which floors, and through which entrance?
- Is there anywhere to walk a dog on or near the property?

Campgrounds, parks and beaches have their own rules — leash requirements, restricted areas, and seasonal closures that vary by site and by season. Check the specific site rather than assuming the general rule. If you are renting rather than staying in a hotel, the same care about written permission applies as it does at home; the [renting guide](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada) makes the argument for getting it in the document.

## Food, water, medication and records

**Bring the food the animal already eats, and bring more than you need.** Switching diets abruptly causes digestive upset, and the brand you rely on may not be stocked where you are going. Measure it out and add a couple of extra days for delays.

**Water.** Carry a bottle and a collapsible bowl, and offer it at every stop. Some animals are fussy about unfamiliar water; bringing a few litres from home for the first day or two is a small effort that avoids a small problem.

**Medication in its original, labelled packaging**, with enough to cover the trip plus a buffer for a delay or an extended stay. Note the name, strength and dose somewhere separate from the bottle, in case the bottle is lost.

**Records.** A summary of vaccination history and any chronic conditions, on paper or as a photo on your phone. If your animal needs care while you are away, the clinic you walk into knows nothing about it, and the file at home is closed on a Sunday.

**A small first-aid kit** for the obvious minor things, and — more usefully — a decision in advance that anything beyond the obvious gets a phone call rather than an attempt.

## Plan the veterinary side of the destination

Do this before you leave, when it takes five minutes and no adrenaline.

- Look up **one regular clinic and one emergency option** near where you are staying. Note the names, addresses, phone numbers and hours.
- Check the drive time from your accommodation.
- Keep your own clinic’s number to hand — they hold the history, and can often speak to another practice directly.
- If you are going somewhere genuinely remote, find out where the nearest veterinary care actually is, because it may be a long way and that changes what you should carry and how you should behave with the animal.

The [emergency preparation guide](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada) covers what to have in the folder; travelling is simply the case where you also need a second set of destinations. If insurance is part of your plan, check what it says about care received away from home before you go — that is a policy-wording question, covered in the [insurance guide](/guides/pet-insurance-in-canada).

## Weather, which in this country is the plan

Canadian travel is weather planning wearing a hat.

**Summer.** Heat is the serious risk. Walk early and late, keep to shade, watch pavement temperature against the back of your hand before a dog walks on it, and treat air conditioning in the car as equipment rather than comfort. Flat-faced dogs and cats tolerate heat poorly and need a more cautious plan than other animals.

**Winter.** Cold, road salt, ice and short daylight all matter, and a long drive can strand you. Carry blankets, water that will not freeze solid, and enough food for an unplanned extra day. The [winter care guide](/guides/winter-dog-care-in-canada) covers paws, coats and the hazards that put dogs in front of a vet.

**Any season, this country.** Highways close. Ferries cancel. Storms arrive. Travel with the assumption that you might be a day late, and carry the food and medication that assumption implies.

## Flights, trains, ferries and buses

Rules for carrying animals are set by each operator, differ between them, differ by route and aircraft, and change. **They are the one part of this guide you should not take from any website, including this one.** Confirm directly with the carrier, for your specific route and date, and get the answer in writing.

What to ask, whoever you are travelling with:

- Are animals accepted on this route at all, in the cabin or otherwise, and for my species and size?
- What are the exact carrier requirements — dimensions, type, ventilation, labelling?
- Is a booking required in advance, and is there a limit per departure?
- What are the fees, and when are they paid?
- What documentation is required, and how recently must anything be dated?
- Are there seasonal or temperature restrictions on the route?
- What happens if the trip is delayed, diverted or cancelled?

Book the animal at the same moment you book yourself, keep every confirmation, and re-confirm close to departure. Then plan the ground portions — getting to and from the terminal is often the harder half.

## The short version

Update the microchip record, restrain the animal properly in the car, get the accommodation policy in an email, and look up a vet at the other end before you need one. Bring the usual food, the labelled medication and a written history. Confirm any carrier’s rules with the carrier, not with a guide. Then plan for a day’s delay, because this is Canada.
