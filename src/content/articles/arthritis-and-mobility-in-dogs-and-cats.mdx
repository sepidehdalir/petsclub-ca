The dog that stopped meeting you at the door did not lose interest. The cat that sleeps on the chair instead of the windowsill did not change its preferences.

Both are usually the same thing, and it is the most under-recognised painful condition in companion animals: osteoarthritis. AAHA has described canine osteoarthritis as underdiagnosed, and the reason is not that it is subtle on an X-ray. It is that animals do not limp until it is advanced, and what they do instead — less, slower, differently — looks exactly like getting old.

Getting old is not a diagnosis, and it is not treatable. This is.

## What owners actually notice

Almost nobody arrives at a clinic saying their animal has joint pain. They arrive saying it has slowed down. The signs are behavioural before they are orthopaedic.

**In dogs**, the veterinary descriptions are specific and recognisable once you know them: slowness to rise, especially after a long rest; getting up front-end-first and then hoisting the back end, or the reverse; difficulty with stairs in either direction; and reduced jumping or play.

Add the ones owners report in ordinary language — lagging on walks that used to be easy, hesitating at the car, sitting down partway through a walk, stiffness that eases after a few minutes of movement then returns, licking persistently at one joint, or becoming irritable about being touched or lifted.

**In cats it is different, and this is the section worth reading twice.**

## Cats hide it, and then hide it some more

A cat with painful joints rarely limps. Cats have a genuine talent for concealing pain, and they also weigh very little relative to their strength, which means they can compensate for a long time without anything looking wrong.

What veterinary sources describe instead is a change in what the cat *does*: weight loss, reduced appetite, a change in general attitude or mood, deteriorating grooming — a scruffy coat, or matting in places the cat can no longer reach — and urinating or defecating outside the litter box. And the one that owners are most likely to see and least likely to interpret: **an inability, or unwillingness, to jump on and off things.**

A cat that used to reach the top of the bookcase and now stops at the chair has told you something specific. A cat that has started missing its landings has told you the same thing more loudly. A cat that has stopped using an upstairs litter box may be reporting a joint problem rather than a box problem — which is why the [house-soiling guide](/guides/litter-box-problems-in-cats) puts medical causes first.

None of that resembles limping, which is why it goes unmentioned at appointments for years.

## What a veterinary assessment adds

Two things you cannot do at home: distinguish osteoarthritis from the several other conditions that produce similar signs, and establish how much pain the animal is actually in.

Expect a physical examination that assesses gait, range of motion, muscle mass and pain response joint by joint. Expect questions about what the animal does at home, which is why a short video of it rising, walking or attempting a jump is genuinely useful — a clinic floor is not where a cat behaves normally. Imaging may follow, and bloodwork often does too, both to look for other causes and because it informs what treatment is appropriate.

Be specific about what has changed and when. "He is slower on the stairs and stopped jumping onto the bed about four months ago" is a far more useful sentence than "she's getting on."

## What treatment looks like, at the level an article can describe

Modern veterinary management of osteoarthritis is **multimodal** — several approaches used together, deliberately, so that no single one carries the whole load. That framing is standard in the current pain management guidelines, and it is worth knowing because it sets an expectation: you should not expect one intervention and a discharge.

The categories, at a high level:

- **Pain management**, which is a prescribing decision made by a veterinarian who has examined the animal. There are several drug classes used in dogs and cats and they differ meaningfully between the two species. This article names none and doses none — that is not editorial caution, it is that the wrong drug or the wrong species is how animals are harmed.
- **Weight management.** Overweight is a recognised risk factor for the development and progression of joint disease, and it is the intervention an owner has the most direct control over.
- **Controlled exercise.** Regular, low-impact, consistent movement to maintain muscle and support the joint, prescribed rather than improvised.
- **Rehabilitation and physical therapy**, where available.
- **Dietary modulation**, including therapeutic diets formulated for joint support.
- **Environmental modification**, which is the next section and the part you can start today.
- **Surgery**, for specific conditions and specific joints.

**On supplements:** joint supplements are widely sold and the evidence base varies considerably between products and ingredients. Ask your veterinarian which, if any, they consider worth using for your animal, rather than choosing from a shelf. A supplement is not a substitute for pain relief in an animal that is in pain.

## What to change at home

This is the highest-value, lowest-cost part, and it does more for an animal's day than most purchases.

**Traction, first and most.** Hard floors are the single biggest household obstacle for an arthritic dog. Runners and mats along the routes the animal actually uses — bed to door, around corners, up to the food bowl — change how confidently it moves. A dog that is afraid of slipping moves less, loses muscle, and gets worse.

**Remove the jumps, and replace them with stages.** Ramps or steps to the sofa, the bed, the car. For cats the instinct is to remove height, and that is the wrong answer — cats still want to be high up, so give them a route in two or three short hops rather than one long one. A stool beside the windowsill restores a place the cat had given up.

**Litter boxes for old joints.** Lower sides, more of them, and on the floor the cat spends its time on. A box requiring a climb and a staircase will eventually be abandoned.

**Bedding.** Supportive, thick, and out of draughts. Cold makes stiff joints harder, which matters here: the [winter guide](/guides/winter-dog-care-in-canada) notes that arthritic and elderly animals have more difficulty on snow and ice and are likelier to slip.

**Bowls at a comfortable height**, so eating does not require holding an uncomfortable position.

**Keep grooming reachable.** A cat that cannot groom its back end needs help with it rather than a reputation for being scruffy.

**Adjust the exercise. Do not stop it.** Shorter and more frequent beats long and occasional, and consistency beats intensity — the weekend hike after five sedentary days is the pattern that causes setbacks. Lost muscle makes a painful joint worse, so rest is not the treatment.

## The long game

Osteoarthritis is managed rather than cured, which changes what success looks like. The goal is a comfortable animal doing the things it wants to do, reassessed as it changes — not a fixed prescription.

That makes it a recurring cost rather than an event, and worth putting in the right column: ongoing medication, repeat examinations and monitoring, sometimes diets, rather than one bill. The [senior guide](/guides/senior-dogs-and-cats) makes the same point about chronic conditions generally, and [how veterinary bills are structured](/guides/what-veterinary-care-costs-in-canada) covers planning for the recurring kind.

It also arrives earlier than people expect in large-breed dogs, which reach the senior life stage years before small ones. Waiting until an animal is visibly old before raising mobility is waiting too long.

## The short version

Animals with joint pain do not limp — they do less. In dogs, watch for slowness to rise, difficulty with stairs, and reduced jumping. In cats, watch for stopped jumping, missed landings, a coat that has stopped being groomed, and a litter box that has been abandoned.

Treat "he's just getting old" as a hypothesis, take a video, and say what changed and when. Treatment is multimodal by design, weight is the lever you control most directly, and never give human pain medication.

Then fix the floors. Traction is free, and it is the change that most reliably gives an animal back its confidence.
