"Six months" was the answer for a long time, and for a lot of animals it still is. But the guidance genuinely changed, it changed in a direction most owners have not heard about, and the part that changed is the part that matters if you have a large-breed puppy.

This is what the decision actually turns on, what it costs you in Canada beyond the invoice, and the questions that make the conversation at your clinic a real one.

## The timing question, and why it moved

For cats and for small dogs, the long-standing answer has held up. The American Animal Hospital Association's canine life stage guidance puts **small-breed dogs — those projected to reach under about 45 lb — at around six months for males, and before the first heat, roughly five to six months, for females.**

For large dogs it is different, and this is the change. **Dogs projected to exceed about 45 lb are now generally advised to be neutered after growth has finished, which usually falls between 9 and 15 months.** For large-breed females the recommended window is wider still — somewhere between 5 and 15 months — and it is explicitly meant to be narrowed by a veterinarian against that individual dog's breed, disease risk and lifestyle rather than applied as a rule.

The reasoning is orthopaedic and oncologic. Sex hormones are part of what tells long bones to stop growing, and removing them early in a dog that has a lot of growing left to do has been associated in some breeds with joint problems and with certain cancers. For a dog that will finish at nine kilograms, the question barely arises. For one that will finish at forty, it does.

**What this is not is a licence to defer indefinitely.** An intact animal carries its own risks — some of them serious, some behavioural, and all of them worth naming out loud at the appointment rather than drifting past. The advice is to time the procedure, not to skip it.

## The Canadian money angle nobody mentions

The procedure has a price, and it varies by clinic, by the animal's size and by whether anything unexpected is found. That figure is worth getting from three local practices rather than from anywhere else — the method is in the [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) cost guides.

But two Canadian specifics change the arithmetic, and both are routinely missed.

**Municipal licence fees are commonly lower for a spayed or neutered animal.** In cities that license pets — Toronto and Calgary both do, annually, cats included — the differential is a recurring annual saving rather than a one-off, and over a fifteen-year life it is not trivial. Your own municipality sets both the fee and the differential, so check its page rather than assuming.

**A rescue or shelter adoption fee frequently already includes it.** That is the single biggest reason an adoption fee and a private sale price cannot be compared directly. Ask what is bundled — spay or neuter, vaccines, deworming, microchip, and for cats often FeLV/FIV testing — before concluding that one route is cheaper. [Adopting in Canada](/guides/adopting-a-pet-in-canada) covers what to establish about the route itself.

Low-cost and subsidised programmes exist in many parts of the country, run variously by humane societies, municipalities and veterinary charities. What is available is intensely local, so ask your municipality and your nearest humane society directly.

## What actually happens

Both procedures are done under general anaesthetic. A spay is abdominal surgery and a neuter usually is not, which is the main reason recovery differs between them.

What a good clinic will do without being asked: a pre-operative examination, a conversation about pre-anaesthetic bloodwork and what it is for, monitoring during the procedure, pain relief that continues after the animal goes home, and written discharge instructions you can actually follow at eleven at night.

What you should expect to be asked: when the animal last ate, what medications it is on, and whether anything has changed since booking.

<Checklist title="Worth asking before you book">

- Given this animal's breed and projected adult weight, when do you recommend doing it, and why that window?
- What are the risks of doing it earlier, and of waiting longer, for this particular animal?
- Do you recommend pre-anaesthetic bloodwork, and what would it change?
- How is pain managed during and after, and what am I sending home with?
- What is the written estimate, and what would make the final figure differ from it?
- How long is the recovery, and what restrictions am I enforcing for how many days?
- What is normal at the incision site, and what is a reason to call you?
- Is there anything about this breed I should know before anaesthesia?

</Checklist>

## Recovery, honestly

The instruction that gets ignored is exercise restriction, and it is the one that matters. A dog that feels fine on day three and is allowed to behave accordingly is how a straightforward recovery becomes a complicated one. Plan for the restriction period before the surgery, not during it — a settled animal that is already comfortable resting in a [crate or pen](/guides/crate-training-a-puppy-in-canada) makes this dramatically easier than one for whom confinement is itself a novel stress.

Watch the incision daily. Keep the animal from licking it, using whatever your clinic sends home rather than improvising.

<VetNote>
Contact your veterinary clinic if the incision opens, bleeds, discharges, or
becomes increasingly red, swollen or painful; if your animal is persistently
lethargic, will not eat, or vomits repeatedly after coming home; if it seems
in pain despite the medication you were given; or if it has not urinated
within the expected period. Do not give any human pain medication — several
are toxic to dogs and cats. If something seems wrong, phoning is free and the
clinic would far rather hear from you. This describes when to call, not what
to do.
</VetNote>

## The behavioural expectations to set aside

Neutering is not a training method. It can reduce some hormonally driven behaviours — roaming and some forms of marking among them — and it does nothing at all for behaviours that are learned or fear-based. A dog that pulls on the lead will still pull. A dog that is anxious when left alone will still be anxious, and that is a [separate project](/guides/separation-anxiety-in-dogs) with its own plan.

Expecting surgery to fix a behaviour problem is how owners end up disappointed by a procedure that did exactly what it was supposed to.

## The short version

For cats and small dogs, the familiar timing broadly still stands. For large-breed dogs the guidance has moved toward waiting until growth is complete — usually 9 to 15 months, and wider and more individual for females. If you have a large-breed puppy, that is a real conversation to have rather than a date to accept.

Get the quote from three local clinics, ask your municipality what the licence differential is, and find out what an adoption fee already covers before you compare it with anything.
