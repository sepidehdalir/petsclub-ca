**Most cats not intended for breeding should be spayed or neutered by five months of age. For dogs, there is no universal age.** The [Canadian Veterinary Medical Association's position](https://www.canadianveterinarians.net/policy-and-outreach/position-statements/statements/spaying-and-neutering-of-dogs-and-cats/) recommends discussing the individual pet's risks and benefits with a veterinarian. Breed, expected size, health and the ability to prevent unplanned mating all matter.

This guide separates the cat and dog decisions, explains what an age range can—and cannot—tell you, and offers questions for a Canadian veterinary appointment.

## The timing question, and why it moved

### Cats: plan the timing early

The CVMA's guidance is specific: **most cats not intended for breeding should have the procedure by five months** to help prevent unwanted pregnancies. Discuss it at kitten appointments rather than waiting for visible heat signs. [AAHA's owner guidance](https://www.aaha.org/resources/spay-or-neuter/) notes that some female cats can enter heat as early as four months. A cat does not need to have a litter first.

Your veterinarian still assesses the individual cat's health and fitness for surgery. Tell the clinic about illness, medication, a current heat or any possibility of pregnancy. If mating may have happened, call promptly rather than waiting for the planned surgery date; our [cat pregnancy guide](/guides/cat-pregnancy-signs-and-tests) explains why signs alone cannot confirm it.

### Dogs: an individual decision, not one age

The [AAHA canine life-stage framework](https://www.aaha.org/resources/life-stage-canine-2019/reproductive-health/) uses projected adult size and sex as starting points. Its size categories divide at approximately 20 kg (45 lb), not at a guaranteed biological turning point. The table below summarises that framework; it is **not an instruction to book surgery at a particular age**.

In the AAHA discussion, earlier sterilisation can reduce unwanted litters and mammary neoplasia; later timing may reduce certain orthopaedic risks and urinary incontinence, termed urethral sphincter mechanism incompetence. These competing considerations require clinical discretion. Delaying also means planning how to prevent mating while the dog remains intact.

[UC Davis's research summary](https://www.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/news/when-should-you-neuter-or-spay-your-dog) describes differences by breed and sex: timing has been associated in some breeds with joint disorders or certain cancers. An association is not proof of cause, a prediction for an individual dog, or a reason to apply one breed's result to all dogs. Bring breed information and expected adult size to the appointment, rather than choosing a date from a chart alone.

Tell the vet about current heat signs, possible mating or false pregnancy before booking. Our [dog pregnancy guide](/guides/dog-pregnancy-signs-and-tests) explains why milk production or nesting does not prove pregnancy.

## The Canadian money angle nobody mentions

Request a written estimate from your clinic. If comparing practices, compare included examination, monitoring, pain relief and follow-up rather than totals alone. Our [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) budget guides provide a worksheet approach; there is no single price that applies to every Canadian pet.

Also check two costs outside the surgical estimate:

**Your municipality may charge different licence fees for altered animals.** Check the current rules where you live; a fee difference is not a medical reason to select a surgery date. [Toronto's pet-licensing page](https://www.toronto.ca/community-people/animals-pets/pet-licensing/) is one local example, not a national fee schedule.

**Ask a shelter or rescue what the adoption fee includes.** Do not assume surgery, vaccines, parasite treatment or identification are included without checking the agreement. [Adopting in Canada](/guides/adopting-a-pet-in-canada) covers questions to ask before committing.

Low-cost and subsidised programmes exist in many parts of the country, run variously by humane societies, municipalities and veterinary charities. What is available is intensely local, so ask your municipality and your nearest humane society directly.

## What actually happens

Both procedures are done under general anaesthetic. A spay is abdominal surgery and a neuter usually is not, which is the main reason recovery differs between them.

Before booking, ask about the pre-operative examination, whether bloodwork is appropriate, anaesthetic monitoring, pain relief and written discharge instructions. Confirm which services the estimate includes.

What you should expect to be asked: when the animal last ate, what medications it is on, and whether anything has changed since booking.

<Checklist title="Worth asking before you book">

- Given this animal's breed and projected adult weight, when do you recommend doing it, and why that window?
- What are the risks of doing it earlier, and of waiting longer, for this particular animal?
- How will we prevent mating while waiting, and do heat signs or possible pregnancy change the plan?
- Do you recommend pre-anaesthetic bloodwork, and what would it change?
- How is pain managed during and after, and what am I sending home with?
- What is the written estimate, and what would make the final figure differ from it?
- How long is the recovery, and what restrictions am I enforcing for how many days?
- What is normal at the incision site, and what is a reason to call you?
- Is there anything about this breed I should know before anaesthesia?

</Checklist>

## Recovery, honestly

Follow the clinic's individual activity restrictions even if your pet seems comfortable. Plan a calm recovery space before surgery, and ask how to manage stairs, jumping, toileting and other pets. If the clinic recommends a crate or pen for a dog, [familiarising them with it beforehand](/guides/crate-training-a-puppy-in-canada) may make that plan easier to follow.

Watch the incision daily. Keep the animal from licking it, using whatever your clinic sends home rather than improvising.

<VetNote>
Contact your veterinary clinic if the incision opens, bleeds, discharges, or
becomes increasingly red, swollen or painful; if your animal is persistently
lethargic, will not eat, or vomits repeatedly after coming home; if it seems
in pain despite the medication you were given; or if it has not urinated
within the expected period. Do not give any human pain medication — several
are toxic to dogs and cats. If something seems wrong, call the clinic or its
after-hours service. This describes when to call, not what
to do.
</VetNote>

## The behavioural expectations to set aside

Neutering is not a guaranteed behaviour treatment. It may reduce some hormone-related behaviours, but it is not a substitute for investigating fear, anxiety, pain or training needs. Describe the specific concern to your veterinary team; [separation-related distress](/guides/separation-anxiety-in-dogs), for example, needs its own assessment and plan.

Expecting surgery to fix a behaviour problem is how owners end up disappointed by a procedure that did exactly what it was supposed to.

## The short version

For most cats not intended for breeding, plan with the clinic for surgery by five months. For dogs, ask for an individual recommendation rather than treating six months—or any other age—as universal. Confirm how to prevent mating while waiting, compare written estimate scope, and organise recovery before the appointment.
