Most advice about where to get a dog or cat is really an argument about ethics, and it usually skips the part a Canadian actually needs: what each route obliges the other side to tell you, what the federal rules now prohibit outright, and which questions separate a serious organisation from a well-designed website.

This is that part. It applies whether you are heading to a municipal shelter, a breed rescue, a breeder or a listing on a classifieds site.

## The four routes, and what each one owes you

**A shelter or humane society.** Municipal or SPCA-run, taking in strays and surrenders. The animal is usually already spayed or neutered, vaccinated and microchipped, and the history is whatever came through the door with it — sometimes detailed, often nothing. What you get in exchange for the uncertainty is an organisation with a physical address, staff who have handled the animal daily, and a return policy.

**A rescue.** Usually volunteer-run, frequently foster-based, often breed- or species-specific. The great advantage is that a foster has watched the animal live in a house — with stairs, a vacuum, a doorbell, sometimes children or another dog — and that is the single most useful information available about any adult animal. Rescues vary enormously in how they operate, which is the reason for the questions further down.

**A breeder.** The right route if you need predictability about adult size, coat or temperament. A serious breeder will let you see where the animals live, show you health testing appropriate to the breed, and ask you more questions than you ask them.

**A private sale.** The route with the fewest structural protections, and the one where the questions below matter most.

## The import rule that changed, and almost nobody knows

This is the most important Canadian fact in this article.

**Since 28 September 2022, commercial dogs from countries the Canadian Food Inspection Agency classifies as high-risk for dog rabies are not permitted to enter Canada, and import permits are not issued for them.**

The word doing the work is *commercial*, and it is broader than it sounds. The CFIA's definition covers dogs imported for resale, **adoption, fostering**, breeding, show, exhibition and research — not only dogs sold for profit. A rescue importing dogs for adoption is squarely inside it.

The measure followed cases in 2021 of dogs entering Canada with dog-variant rabies, after which the Public Health Agency of Canada and provincial public health authorities asked the CFIA to act. The disease is effectively always fatal once signs appear, and — the detail that makes paperwork inadequate on its own — an infected dog may not look unwell.

What this means practically: if an organisation is offering dogs brought in from abroad, where those dogs came from is a question with a legal answer, not a matter of preference. Ask it directly, and check the CFIA's current list of high-risk countries rather than taking anyone's word for the classification.

## The questions the CFIA tells you to ask

These come from the CFIA's own guidance for Canadians acquiring a dog, and they work just as well on a breeder as on a rescue.

<Checklist title="Ask any seller or organisation">

- **Where are the dogs from?** For a breeder: credentials, certifications and references. For a rescue: the animal's history, and if it was imported, from where and under what permit.
- **What documentation comes with it?** Vaccination records, veterinary history, and a health certificate covering age, rabies vaccination, core vaccination, and parasite testing and treatment.
- **Can you show safe, compliant transport** if the animal is being moved any distance?
- **May I visit and see where the animals are kept?**
- **What happens if there is a health problem afterwards** — is there a return policy or any medical support?

</Checklist>

And the warning signs the CFIA names, which are worth knowing because each one is individually easy to rationalise:

- Offering to meet somewhere neutral instead of letting you visit
- Preventing you from seeing the animal, or its mother, in person
- Asking for money transferred abroad
- Not asking you anything about your household or lifestyle
- Providing no documents and no health guarantee

The last one on that list is the one people forgive most readily and should forgive least. An organisation that asks you nothing has told you something about how it places animals.

<Note title="On buying online">
The CFIA is direct about impulse purchases arranged over the internet: they
support puppy mills and carry a real risk of acquiring a sick animal. A photo
and a payment link are not a process. If you cannot see where an animal has
been living, you do not know what you are taking on.
</Note>

## What a good process feels like from your side

Counterintuitively, the harder it is to adopt, the better the sign. An application form, a conversation about your housing and hours, a reference check, sometimes a home visit — these read as obstacles and function as evidence that the organisation cares where the animal ends up.

What you should be given in return: honest information about the animal's known behaviour, including the parts that make it harder to place. A rescue that describes every dog as good with children, cats and other dogs is either extraordinarily lucky or not telling you things.

Ask what the animal is like at three in the morning, on its own, and in a thunderstorm. Ask what the foster would change about the household it goes to. Ask what has already been tried.

## Before you commit, settle these

- **Your housing.** Whether you are allowed the animal at all is provincial and, in a condo, doubled — a no-pets clause is void in Ontario and binding in British Columbia and Quebec. The [renting guide](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada) works through it. Establishing this before an adoption application, not after, is the kindest possible sequencing.
- **The money, honestly.** Not the fee — the four-figure unplanned bill. The [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) cost guides both make the same argument: the monthly cost is rarely what causes the crisis.
- **Insurance timing.** If you are going to insure, do it before there is a medical history, because the cover you can buy narrows with every entry in the file. The [insurance guide](/guides/pet-insurance-in-canada) explains why pre-existing is broader than people expect.
- **What is already included in the fee** — spay or neuter, vaccines, deworming, microchip, and for cats often FeLV/FIV testing. This is what makes an adoption fee incomparable with a purchase price.
- **A veterinary practice**, chosen before you need one. [How to choose one](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada) is a decision worth an afternoon.

## The paperwork that is actually yours to do

Whatever route you take, two things are yours alone and both get missed.

**The microchip registration.** Implanting and registering are separate steps. An animal can arrive microchipped and still be, functionally, untraceable — because the record points at the rescue, the breeder or a previous owner. Get the registry name, log in, and put your own name, address and current phone number on it. This is the single highest-value ten minutes in the whole process.

**Municipal licensing.** Some Canadian cities license cats as well as dogs — Toronto and Calgary both do, indoor cats included, and Calgary's threshold is three months of age. Check your own municipality's animal bylaw rather than assuming it does not apply to you.

## Then the first month

The work starts when the animal arrives, and it is different by species: a puppy's first month is a [schedule](/guides/bringing-home-a-puppy-first-30-days), a kitten's is [a set of decisions about rooms](/guides/bringing-home-a-kitten-first-30-days).

For an adult animal from a shelter or rescue, add one thing to both: give it considerably longer than you think to become itself. The animal you meet in week one is not the animal you will have in month three, and that is true in both directions.

## The short version

Every route has a version done well and a version done badly, and the questions that tell them apart are the same questions in each case: where is this animal from, may I see where it has been living, what documentation comes with it, and what happens if something goes wrong.

Know the import rule — commercial dogs, adoption and fostering included, cannot come from countries the CFIA lists as high-risk for dog rabies. Establish your housing before you apply. Re-register the microchip in your own name the week the animal arrives.

And treat a difficult application process as a recommendation rather than an obstacle.
