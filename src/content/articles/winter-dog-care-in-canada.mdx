For winter walks in Canada, plan a short route you can leave early, check the surface as well as the forecast, and clean and inspect your dog's paws afterwards. Coat and boot decisions depend on the individual dog and the conditions—not a single Canada-wide temperature chart. Suspected poisoning, frostbite or serious cold-related illness needs veterinary help, not another item of winter gear.

A wet Vancouver walk, an icy Montréal pavement and a Winnipeg cold snap need different plans. This guide helps you decide what to do before, during and after an outing; the separate [dog-boots buying guide](/guides/choosing-dog-boots-in-canada) covers product measurements and fitting.

## There is no single “too cold” number

Every winter, some version of a chart circulates that maps temperatures to risk. It is a tempting thing to want. A chart cannot establish a safe walking time for every dog. Consider these factors together:

- **Coat and body condition.** Short-haired and lean dogs may need more protection; a thick coat is not permission for unlimited exposure.
- **Size.** Small dogs may need a different outing from larger companions.
- **Age and health.** Ask your veterinary team about your puppy, senior dog or dog with a medical condition. Mobility problems also change which surfaces and routes are manageable.
- **Usual routine.** Do not assume a dog used to brief toilet breaks is ready for a long snowy hike.
- **Wet and windy conditions.** Rain, slush and wind change the outing even when the thermometer reading has not changed.

Use the forecast and conditions alongside your dog's response; neither a thermometer nor enthusiasm alone establishes that an outing is safe.

## What “too cold” looks like on your dog

Watch for a change from your dog’s usual comfortable movement and behaviour, including:

- shivering, or a tucked, hunched posture with the tail clamped
- lifting one paw, then another — or stopping to hold a paw up
- slowing down, lagging behind, or turning to face home
- whining at the door, or refusing to go further
- anxious, distracted behaviour from a dog who is normally happy to plod

Head back to shelter rather than pushing through discomfort. Paw lifting or limping can also mean a paw problem; inspect gently and contact your veterinary team if pain or changed movement persists.

<VetNote>
If your dog becomes weak, confused or collapses in the cold, contact an emergency veterinary clinic immediately. Move them into a warm, dry place while arranging help. Suspected frostbite also needs prompt veterinary attention; it can affect paws, ears and the tail, and visible changes may be delayed. Do not rub the area, use a hair dryer or heating pad, or risk thawing and refreezing it. Follow the clinic’s instructions for transport. [VCA’s frostbite guidance](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/frostbite-in-dogs) explains why seemingly minor skin changes need assessment.
</VetNote>

## Road salt and ice melts: a post-walk routine

Road salt is one winter paw hazard. The [Canadian Veterinary Medical Association](https://www.canadianveterinarians.net/related-resources/cold-weather-and-pets) notes that salt trapped between the toes can cause irritation and inflammation.

Do not treat “pet-friendly” as “safe to eat.” The [ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center](https://www.aspca.org/news/keeping-pets-safe-during-winter-dangers-ice-melts) warns that even ingredients used in pet-friendly products can cause stomach upset. Risk depends on the product and exposure. Keep bags inaccessible, discourage eating treated snow and prevent paw licking until residue is cleaned away. Contact your veterinarian if you suspect ingestion; keep the packaging available to identify the product.

What actually works:

- **Rinse or wipe after every walk** — paws first, including between the toes, but also the legs and the belly, which is where a low-slung dog picks up most of what is on the ground. Keep a towel and lukewarm water ready by the door, then dry gently.
- **Ask a groomer or veterinary team about paw hair** if snow repeatedly collects there; do not cut close to irritated skin.
- **Ask about a suitable paw protectant.** It does not replace cleaning or veterinary assessment of sore or damaged skin.
- **Boots, when they fit and can be used comfortably.** They are one protective measure, not a guarantee against salt exposure or every winter hazard. Keep inspecting the paws and cleaning away residue after walks.
- **Inspect after the walk** for redness, splits, bleeding or material caught between toes.

### Antifreeze is an urgent exposure, not a wait-and-see problem

Ethylene glycol antifreeze can cause fatal poisoning. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/special-pet-topics/poisoning/ethylene-glycol-antifreeze-poisoning) explains that treatment becomes less successful as time passes, and apparent improvement does not mean the danger has passed. If you suspect your dog licked it, call a veterinarian or emergency clinic immediately rather than waiting for signs. Keep containers secured and pets away from spills while cleaning them up. Save the clinic number in advance using our [emergency-vet planning guide](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada).

## Ice balls, crusted snow and the other paw problems

Snow can collect in paw hair and form uncomfortable ice balls. The [CVMA recommends warm water](https://www.canadianveterinarians.net/related-resources/cold-weather-and-pets) to help melt and dislodge them. Do not pull at stuck material or assume that every limp is an ice ball. Persistent pain, bleeding or refusal to bear weight needs a veterinary call.

Choose cleared routes where possible, and turn back if sharp crust or slippery footing makes movement difficult. A shorter route with several easy exits is more useful than committing to a distant turnaround point.

## Coats and boots: decide before buying

A coat can be useful for a short-haired or lean dog, but fit and the conditions matter. Ask your veterinary team about additional protection for a very young, older or unwell dog. Check that clothing allows normal movement and toileting, inspect for rubbing, and replace wet items with dry ones. Clothing does not make an otherwise unsuitable walk safe.

Choose boots using the exact model's measuring and fitting instructions. Some winter soles are specified for outdoor use only, so the location of an initial trial also depends on the product. Stop if the fit appears uncomfortable, and ask the veterinary team about injured paws or changed movement. ## The dark is a separate problem

By December much of the country is walking dogs in the dark at both ends of the day. Snowbanks narrow sidewalks and block sightlines, drivers are looking at a windshield they have half-cleared, and a dark dog on a dark leash on an unlit street is genuinely hard to see.

<Note title="Winter visibility, in three items">
A reflective or LED collar, a reflective leash, and something reflective on you.
Keep your dog leashed near roads and choose well-lit routes where possible.
</Note>

## Frozen water is the one to be inflexible about

Avoid frozen lakes, rivers, retention ponds and stormwater channels. The CVMA warns that ice may not support a dog’s weight.

Keep the dog leashed away from frozen water and do not throw toys onto ice. If an animal falls through, call emergency services rather than putting yourself onto unsafe ice.

## Keeping a workable routine when walks get shorter

Plan indoor activities alongside the outdoor exercise your dog can comfortably manage. There is no reliable conversion such as ten minutes of scent work equalling a particular walking distance. Choose activities your dog enjoys and can do safely:

- Search for a few pieces of their usual food in an easy-to-clean space.
- Practise familiar reward-based cues in short sessions, stopping before frustration.
- Offer a suitable supervised food toy, counting the food within the usual allowance.
- Keep a quiet resting place available as well as opportunities to interact.

Avoid making stairs, jumping or slippery-floor games the default replacement for a walk, especially when mobility is a concern. Ask the veterinary team for an activity plan if pain, illness or a recent procedure limits movement. New restlessness is not enough to diagnose either boredom or overtiredness.

## Itchy skin is not automatically “just winter”

Cold weather and indoor conditions can coincide with dry, irritated skin, but the season does not diagnose the cause. Ask your veterinarian about a bathing routine and suitable products rather than repeatedly changing shampoos. Persistent itching, sores, hair loss or chewing at one area deserves assessment. The [ASPCA’s cold-weather guidance](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/general-pet-care/cold-weather-safety-tips) includes washing and drying paws and asking the vet about bathing products.

## A repeatable winter-walk checklist

**Before leaving:** check the forecast and ground, choose an easy-return route, and have a towel and rinse water ready. **Outside:** keep watching the dog, avoid treated snow and frozen water, and turn back when comfort or footing deteriorates. **Back home:** clean and dry paws, inspect skin and equipment, and call your vet about persistent pain or suspected exposure.
