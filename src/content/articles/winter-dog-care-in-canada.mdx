Canadian winter is not one thing. A February afternoon in Victoria and a February afternoon in Winnipeg ask completely different questions of the same dog. What follows is not a set of temperature rules — those do not survive contact with a country this size — but the small number of things that actually decide whether your dog is fine outside, and the handful of winter hazards that send Canadian dogs to a vet every year.

## There is no single “too cold” number

Every winter, some version of a chart circulates that maps temperatures to risk. It is a tempting thing to want. It is also close to useless, because the same reading means different things depending on five variables that vary enormously between dogs:

- **Coat.** A double coat — a dense insulating undercoat beneath a weather-shedding topcoat — is a different garment from a single coat. A husky, a Bernese, a Newfoundland and a Canadian Eskimo Dog are dressed for this. A greyhound, a boxer, a vizsla and a whippet are not, and no amount of enthusiasm changes that.
- **Size and body mass.** Small dogs lose heat faster than large ones for the same reason a mug of coffee cools faster than a pot: more surface area relative to volume. A four-kilogram dog and a forty-kilogram dog are not having the same walk.
- **Age and health.** Puppies regulate temperature poorly. Arthritic and elderly dogs have a harder time on snow and ice and are likelier to slip. And a specific point worth knowing rather than guessing at: dogs with diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease or a hormonal condition such as Cushing's have more difficulty regulating body temperature at all, which makes the cold a different proposition for them than for the dog next to them on the same walk.
- **Acclimatisation.** A dog that has been outside for an hour a day since October is not the same dog as one that has spent three months on a heated floor.
- **Wet and wind, not just cold.** Dry −20 °C with no wind is genuinely easier than −8 °C with sleet and an open-field wind. Wet fur stops insulating.

The practical version: stop reading the thermometer and start reading the dog.

## What “too cold” looks like on your dog

Dogs are not subtle about this once you know the sequence. Watch for:

- shivering, or a tucked, hunched posture with the tail clamped
- lifting one paw, then another — or stopping to hold a paw up
- slowing down, lagging behind, or turning to face home
- whining at the door, or refusing to go further
- anxious, distracted behaviour from a dog who is normally happy to plod

Any of these means the walk is over. Not “one more block.”

<VetNote>
Frostbite and hypothermia are real and they are veterinary problems, not
home-remedy problems. Frostbite affects the poorly insulated extremities first
— the ears, the feet and the tail. Affected skin may look pale or red, and be
swollen and painful; in severe cases the tissue turns black and hard and
eventually sloughs away. Hypothermia is harder to read than people expect,
because shivering is not the reliable signal in dogs that it is in us: look
instead for weakness or disorientation, shallow breathing, a slow pulse, and at
worst collapse. If you think you are seeing either, get the dog dry and out of
the cold and call a veterinarian or your nearest emergency clinic. Do not rub
or massage a frostbitten area, and do not put dry heat on it — no hair dryer,
no heating pad. Warm water rather than hot is the safe medium. And do not
attempt to warm a frostbitten area outdoors if you cannot then keep it warm,
because refreezing does more damage than the original injury. This article
describes what to look for so you know when to call. It does not tell you how
to treat it.
</VetNote>

## Road salt is the hazard most owners underestimate

For dogs in Canadian cities, the single most common winter problem is not the cold. It is what we put on the ground to deal with the cold.

Sodium chloride and the calcium and magnesium chlorides in commercial de-icers do two things to a dog. They abrade and dry out the paw pads, which cracks them; and they get licked off, because a dog with irritated feet will chew at them.

**And “pet-safe” is not a regulated claim.** There are no labelling requirements a product must meet before it can be sold as pet-safe or pet-friendly, which means the phrase carries no guarantee at all. The usual substitutes are urea and magnesium chloride, and neither is inert: urea can cause drooling and stomach upset, and in a large ingestion worse than that; magnesium chloride is a gastrointestinal irritant. Both can still damage paw pads underfoot. Treat the gentler products as gentler, and keep doing the same things you would with rock salt.

What actually works:

- **Rinse or wipe after every walk** — paws first, including between the toes, but also the legs and the belly, which is where a low-slung dog picks up most of what is on the ground. A shallow tray of lukewarm water by the door is faster than it sounds and becomes automatic within a week.
- **Keep the hair between the pads trimmed level with the pad.** Long tufts hold salt, grit and ice.
- **Use a paw balm** before the walk as a partial barrier, and to help pads that are already dry.
- **Boots, if your dog will genuinely wear them.** A well-fitted boot is the only complete answer to salt. A badly fitted boot that comes off in a snowbank is not.
- **Check the pads under good light** once a week for splits, redness or bleeding.

And the one that is not about paws at all: **antifreeze**. Ethylene glycol tastes sweet, dogs will drink it from a driveway puddle, and the lethal dose is small — a few teaspoons for a dog, one or two for a cat. Severe kidney failure follows within roughly 36 to 72 hours in dogs and within 12 to 24 hours in cats, and there is a cruel middle stage where a dog looks to have recovered about half a day in. Do not wait that out. Wipe up garage spills immediately, keep containers sealed and out of reach, and if you have any reason to think your dog has licked antifreeze, call a veterinarian or an emergency clinic **now** rather than waiting to see whether symptoms appear — the treatment window is the thing you are racing. [What to have ready before a night like that](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada) is worth an hour of an ordinary evening.

## Ice balls, crusted snow and the other paw problems

Wet snow packs into the hair between the toes and freezes into hard balls that force the toes apart. It hurts, and it is why a dog suddenly starts limping halfway around the block. Trimming the foot hair is the prevention; warm water is the fix, not pulling.

The other one is crust. When a thaw is followed by a hard freeze, the snowpack develops a sharp crust that a running dog breaks through with every stride. It cuts pads and dew claws. If the surface has that glassy, breakable look, keep it short and stay on cleared paths.

## Coats and boots: who actually needs them

There is a strong instinct in Canada to treat dog coats as a bit silly. For a Bernese in Ottawa, mostly yes. For a lot of dogs, no.

Reach for a coat if your dog is **short-coated, small, very lean, very young, elderly, recently clipped, or recovering from illness or surgery**. A coat should cover the chest and belly — the parts closest to the ground and thinnest on fur — and should not restrict the shoulders. Waterproof matters more than thick: a soaked coat is worse than no coat.

Boots are worth the effort in cities with heavy salting, on crusted snow, and for dogs with pad problems. Fit them properly, then spend a week introducing them indoors with treats before you expect a dog to walk a block in them. Most boot failures are training failures.

## The dark is a separate problem

By December much of the country is walking dogs in the dark at both ends of the day. Snowbanks narrow sidewalks and block sightlines, drivers are looking at a windshield they have half-cleared, and a dark dog on a dark leash on an unlit street is genuinely hard to see.

<Note title="Winter visibility, in three items">
A reflective or LED collar, a reflective leash, and something reflective on you.
Drivers see the human first. If your dog walks off-leash anywhere near a road,
the dark is the season to stop.
</Note>

## Frozen water is the one to be inflexible about

Every winter, dogs go through the ice on lakes, rivers, retention ponds and stormwater channels — and every winter, people go in after them. Moving water and municipal stormwater ponds are the worst offenders because they stay thin in places that look identical to the rest.

There is no version of this where you assess the ice yourself and get it right consistently. Keep the dog leashed near any frozen water body, do not throw anything onto ice, and if a dog does go through, call 911 rather than going in.

## Keeping a dog tired when walks get short

The hardest part of a Canadian winter is not the cold. It is that four short walks do not empty a dog the way one long one did in September, and an under-exercised dog invents its own projects.

Mental work is disproportionately tiring, and it happens indoors:

- **Scatter feeding and scent games.** Hide a portion of dinner around a room and let the dog find it. Ten minutes of nose work is worth a lot of walking.
- **Food puzzles and a frozen stuffed toy.** Feed a meal out of one instead of a bowl.
- **Short, frequent training sessions.** Five minutes, three times a day, on something new. Teaching a genuinely novel behaviour is far more tiring than rehearsing a known one.
- **Sniffaris.** Same short route, but you let the dog set the pace and stop wherever it wants. A twenty-minute sniffing walk beats a twenty-minute marching walk for settling a dog.
- **Structured indoor movement** — stairs, tug with rules, a hallway recall game — for dogs whose bodies can take it.
- **Enforced rest.** Counter-intuitive, but a great deal of winter restlessness is an overtired dog rather than an under-exercised one. A dog with a settled place to switch off handles a shut-in season far better; [crate training](/guides/crate-training-a-puppy-in-canada) is how that place gets built.

## Dry indoor air, dull coats and itchy skin

Heated indoor air in January is extremely dry, and a lot of “winter allergies” in dogs are simply that. Bathing less often in winter, using a conditioning shampoo when you do, brushing regularly to distribute oils, and running a humidifier in the room the dog sleeps in all help. Persistent itching, scabs, hair loss or a dog that will not leave one spot alone is worth a veterinary appointment rather than another shampoo — it is often something specific and treatable.

## The short version

Watch the dog rather than the thermometer. Rinse the paws every single time. Treat antifreeze and frozen water as absolutes rather than judgement calls. Give a short-coated dog a proper coat and stop feeling odd about it. And replace the distance you have lost with nose work, because the dog does not actually care whether it is tired from walking or from thinking.

Then do the same reading in reverse six months later. [Summer](/guides/summer-heat-safety-for-dogs-in-canada) is the more dangerous of the two seasons, because heat does not give you the warning cold does.
