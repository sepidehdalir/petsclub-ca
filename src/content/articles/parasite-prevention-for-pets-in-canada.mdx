Ask two Canadian owners what parasite prevention their dog is on and when, and you will often get two confident and completely different answers. Both may be right. This is one of the few areas of pet care where the correct answer genuinely changes between Winnipeg and Vancouver, and between May and November in the same city.

What follows is the shape of the problem — which parasites matter where, what actually drives the timing, and the questions that get you a plan for your own postal code rather than a plan copied from an American website.

## Heartworm: the one with a date on it

Heartworm is spread by mosquitoes. The larvae a mosquito picks up have to develop inside the mosquito before that mosquito can infect a dog, and that development is temperature-dependent: it stops below roughly 14 °C. Enough warm days have to accumulate before any mosquito in your area is capable of transmitting anything at all.

That is why Canada has a start date rather than a season of vigilance. **The convention here has long been to begin prevention on 1 June**, and work published in 2025 by the Ontario Veterinary College's Centre for Public Health and Zoonoses looked at whether a warming climate had moved that date. Across 1996 to 2023 it found no significant overall trend toward earlier transmission, though individual locations varied. The conclusion was that the existing approach still holds.

Two things follow, and they are the parts owners get wrong.

**Starting on time is not the same as starting early.** Preventives work retroactively within a window — they kill the larval stages an animal has already picked up in the preceding weeks rather than repelling mosquitoes. That is precisely why a June start is adequate rather than reckless.

**Heartworm is uncommon in Canada, and that is not a reason to skip it.** Prevalence fell nationally through the late twentieth century, though increases were identified in tested dogs in Manitoba and Quebec between 2007 and 2016. The disease is rare and severe, which is the risk profile where prevention is most worth its cost — and the calculation changes entirely if your dog travels. A dog that summers in a region with real transmission, or one adopted from outside the country, is a different animal from a lifelong resident of a cold city.

## Ticks: where they actually are

This is where a US-written article will mislead you, because the Canadian map is specific and it is moving.

**Blacklegged ticks — the vector for Lyme disease — are established in southern Manitoba, southern and southeastern Ontario, southern Quebec, and Nova Scotia.** Those are the areas where a large share of locally acquired human cases originate. In British Columbia the relevant species is the western blacklegged tick, and the risk there has been comparatively stable rather than climbing.

The important qualifier: **established populations in central and eastern Canada have been expanding in both range and number**, and climate is the driver. A region that carried no meaningful risk fifteen years ago may carry it now. This is the single strongest argument for asking a local clinic rather than relying on anything you read once and remembered.

**Ticks are not a summer problem.** They are active whenever it is above freezing, which in much of the country includes long stretches of spring and autumn and some of winter. The mistake is not failing to use prevention in July. It is stopping in September.

If your dog walks in long grass, leaf litter, brush or woodland edges anywhere in the established zones, a tick check after every walk is the habit worth building — run your hands over the whole body, and pay attention to ears, neck, armpits, groin and between the toes.

## Fleas: the indoor parasite

Fleas do not care what the weather is doing, because the part of the life cycle that matters is happening in your house. Eggs and larvae persist in carpet, bedding and floor cracks at indoor temperatures all year, which is why a flea problem that appears in February is not mysterious.

The practical consequences: treating one animal in a multi-pet household usually fails, treating the animal without dealing with the environment usually fails, and an indoor-only cat is not automatically exempt — fleas arrive on people, on other pets, and in second-hand furniture.

## Cats are not small dogs here

Two points that matter more than owners expect.

**Never apply a dog product to a cat without veterinary direction.** Some canine tick preventives contain compounds that are seriously toxic to cats, and the exposure does not require the cat to be treated — a cat that grooms a recently treated dog can be affected. If you have both species in one house, raise it explicitly at the appointment.

**Indoor-only lowers exposure, it does not zero it.** The same reasoning as with vaccination: cats get out, other animals come in, households change. What an indoor cat needs is a real conversation rather than an assumption, and it is covered in the [kitten vaccination guide](/guides/kitten-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) from the other direction.

## What to ask, and why the answer is local

Bring specifics to the appointment. The difference between a useful recommendation and a generic one is entirely in what you tell them.

<Checklist title="Worth asking at your clinic">

- Are blacklegged ticks established where I live, and has that changed recently?
- Which months should this animal be covered for ticks, and when should I stop — if I should?
- When do you want heartworm prevention started, and does it run to a fixed end date?
- Does my dog need a heartworm test first, and how often after that?
- We have a cat and a dog in the same house — which products are safe with both?
- My animal swims, hikes, goes to a cottage, or travels to another province. Does any of that change the plan?
- Is this animal's vaccination profile affected by where we walk — Lyme, for instance?
- What do I do if I find an attached tick, and do you want to see it?

</Checklist>

## If you find a tick

Remove it promptly rather than perfectly. Grasp it as close to the skin as you can with fine tweezers or a tick remover, pull steadily without twisting or crushing the body, and clean the area afterwards. Do not apply heat, petroleum jelly or anything else intended to make it back out — those methods are slower and no better.

Note the date and where you were. If your animal becomes unwell in the following weeks, that detail turns a vague history into a useful one.

<VetNote>
Contact your veterinary clinic if a tick has been attached for some time, if
the site becomes inflamed or infected, or if your animal develops lameness,
lethargy, fever, reduced appetite or swollen joints in the weeks after a known
bite. Contact them straight away if any animal shows tremors, drooling,
twitching, unsteadiness or seizures after a topical parasite product is
applied — and tell them the exact product and when it was used. This describes
what to look for so you know when to call; it does not diagnose or treat
anything.
</VetNote>

## Where this fits with everything else

Parasite prevention is a running cost rather than a one-off, and it scales with body weight because most products are dosed by size — which is one reason a large dog costs multiples of a small one to keep. Both the [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) cost guides put it in the recurring column.

It is also one of the questions worth settling at the very first appointment, alongside the vaccination plan — for a [new puppy](/guides/bringing-home-a-puppy-first-30-days) or a [new kitten](/guides/bringing-home-a-kitten-first-30-days). And if you have not yet chosen a practice, [that decision comes first](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada), because the local knowledge is the product you are buying.

## The short version

Heartworm has a date: 1 June is the Canadian convention, and the reason is arithmetic about mosquitoes and temperature rather than tradition. Ticks have a map, and it is expanding — blacklegged ticks are established in southern Manitoba, southern and southeastern Ontario, southern Quebec and Nova Scotia, with a different species and a steadier picture in British Columbia. Fleas have neither, because they live in your house.

The mistake almost nobody makes is forgetting prevention in July. The mistake a great many people make is stopping in September.
