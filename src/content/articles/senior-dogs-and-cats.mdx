The most expensive sentence in pet ownership is "he's just getting old."

It is expensive because it is usually wrong, and because it delays. A great deal of what owners file under ageing — stiffness, weight loss, confusion, drinking more, sleeping in a different place, becoming grumpy about being touched — is not ageing. It is a condition with a name, and a fair number of those conditions are manageable for years once somebody has identified them.

The animal genuinely is getting old. That is not the same as nothing being wrong.

## When "senior" actually starts

There is no single age, and the reason is that lifespan varies enormously by species and by size.

The 2023 AAHA senior care guidance frames it as **the last 25% of an animal's estimated lifespan** — a proportion rather than a number, which is the only framing that survives contact with a chihuahua and a great dane in the same waiting room. In practice a **large dog can be senior at seven or eight**, while a **small breed may not be until around twelve.**

For cats it is more settled: the feline life stage guidance puts **senior at over ten years old.**

The practical consequence of the proportional rule is one most owners of big dogs have never been told: **a large dog is a senior patient years earlier than feels right.** If your seven-year-old shepherd is still being seen on the same schedule as a three-year-old, that is the conversation to have.

## What actually changes, and what it might really be

The pattern worth internalising is that almost every "old age" sign has a differential behind it.

- **Slowing down, stiffness, reluctance on stairs or jumping up.** Frequently osteoarthritis, which is common, painful and treatable. A cat that has stopped jumping to a favourite perch has told you something specific — cats rarely limp, and they get assessed for arthritis far less often than dogs.
- **Drinking and urinating more.** Not a normal part of ageing. It is one of the more useful signs an owner can notice, and it points at several conditions worth identifying early.
- **Weight change in either direction.** Loss in an older cat especially warrants an appointment rather than relief.
- **Confusion, disrupted sleep, standing in corners, vocalising at night, or forgetting a familiar routine.** There is a recognised cognitive syndrome in older dogs and cats, and it is worth raising rather than absorbing.
- **Reduced hearing or sight.** Manageable — keep furniture where it is, approach within their field of view, use vibration or a torch rather than sudden touch.
- **Bad breath, dropping food, chewing on one side.** Dental disease, which by this age is close to universal and is [the most under-treated painful condition](/guides/dental-care-for-dogs-and-cats) in the population.
- **Becoming irritable about handling or being lifted.** Usually pain rather than temperament.
- **Lumps.** Have them checked rather than watched. Most are nothing; that is a determination somebody else makes.

## What changes at the clinic

More frequent examinations is the whole strategy, and the reason is arithmetic: an animal that ages several years for each of ours can develop and progress a condition entirely within the gap between annual visits.

What a senior visit typically adds beyond the standard examination is baseline bloodwork and urine testing — the point of which is comparison rather than a single verdict. A value inside the normal range that has moved a long way from where that animal sat two years ago is information a one-off test cannot give you. Starting the series *before* anything is wrong is what makes it useful.

Worth asking at the next appointment:

<Checklist title="For the senior examination">

- Given this animal's size and breed, is it a senior patient now — and how often should I be bringing it in?
- What baseline bloodwork would you recommend, and what are we watching over time?
- Should we be assessing for arthritis? What would we look for at home?
- What does this animal's mouth need, and when?
- Is the current weight right, and is the food still appropriate for this life stage?
- What is a realistic expectation for the next couple of years with this breed?
- Which changes should bring me straight back rather than waiting for the next visit?
- What can I change at home to make this easier?

</Checklist>

## What changes at home

Small environmental changes do more for an older animal's quality of life than almost anything you can buy.

**Traction.** Hard floors are the single most common household problem for an arthritic dog. Runners and mats along the routes it actually uses — bed to door, around corners — change how confidently it moves.

**Height, removed and added.** Ramps or steps to the sofa, the bed or the car. For cats, the opposite problem: they still want height, so give them a route up in stages rather than one jump. A senior cat that has abandoned its cat tree has usually not lost interest in being high up.

**Litter boxes for old joints.** Lower sides, more of them, and closer to where the cat spends its time. A cat that has to climb into a deep box and walk the length of the house to reach it will eventually stop doing one of those things.

**Warmth and bedding.** Thicker, supportive, and out of draughts. Cold is harder on old joints, which matters here — the [winter guide](/guides/winter-dog-care-in-canada) notes that arthritic and elderly animals have a harder time on snow and ice and are more likely to slip.

**Keep the brain working.** Shorter, more frequent walks; scent games instead of distance; and for cats, gentler versions of the same [enrichment](/guides/indoor-cat-enrichment-canadian-homes) that a young cat gets. Cognitive decline is worsened by having nothing to do.

**Do not stop the exercise.** Adjust it. An older dog that becomes sedentary loses muscle it needs to support painful joints, and that accelerates the problem.

## The money, honestly

Both cost guides make the same point from different directions: the senior years are where the veterinary spending sits, and for cats especially, a long indoor life means more of them.

Two things follow, and both are decisions made earlier than this article's readers would like.

**Insurance narrows with age and with history.** Premiums commonly rise as an animal ages, and pre-existing conditions are excluded — which means the cover available to a ten-year-old with a file is not the cover that was available at one. If you are reading this while your animal is still young, that is the actionable part; the [insurance guide](/guides/pet-insurance-in-canada) explains why the file matters more than the birthday.

**Ongoing management is a different shape of cost from a crisis.** Chronic conditions in older animals mean medication, prescription diets and repeated monitoring rather than a single bill — a recurring line rather than an emergency. Budget it as one. The [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) cost guides both put it in the right column.

It is also worth having your after-hours plan current, because it stops being hypothetical in these years. [Where you would go at two in the morning](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada) is a fifteen-minute job on an ordinary evening.

## Say what you are worried about

The most useful thing you can bring to a senior appointment is not a symptom list. It is a straight sentence about what you have noticed and what you are afraid it means.

Veterinarians are used to this conversation and are generally much better at it than owners expect. Saying "she has stopped coming upstairs and I do not know if that is her hips or something worse" gets you a better appointment than answering a series of questions about appetite.

And if cost is a constraint, say so plainly. A veterinarian who knows the budget can tell you which tests would actually change the plan and which would not — a conversation that only works before the decisions are made.

## The short version

Senior is a proportion, not a birthday: the last quarter of expected lifespan, which makes a large dog senior at seven or eight and a cat at ten. What changes then is the monitoring — more frequent examinations and baseline bloodwork you can compare against later.

Treat "he's just getting old" as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, because stiffness, thirst, weight change and confusion all have names and several of them are manageable.

Then fix the floors, lower the litter box, and keep the walks short rather than stopping them.
