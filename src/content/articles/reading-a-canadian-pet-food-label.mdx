**Start with the species, nutritional adequacy statement and life stage, then compare calories and feeding amounts.** The ingredient list alone cannot tell you which food is best for your dog or cat. This guide helps you collect comparable information before buying, without ranking brands or prescribing a diet.

Canadian pet-food oversight is divided between several responsibilities. Three federal bodies touch it, and the Competition Bureau’s own guide sets out what each one does: the CFIA regulates the movement of inedible meat products and certification of certain imported pet foods containing animal products; Health Canada administers the prohibition on unsubstantiated health claims; and the Competition Bureau administers the requirement that a prepackaged retail pet food carry a bilingual common name, a metric net quantity declaration and a dealer name and address. Those roles should not be confused with a nutritional adequacy statement: that statement is not Canadian government approval of a diet.

The [Competition Bureau guide](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/education-and-outreach/publications/guide-labelling-and-advertising-pet-foods) distinguishes mandatory packaging declarations from its broader labelling recommendations. The guide itself is not law.

For ingredient definitions, the guide points to an American organisation. Ingredient definitions come from the **Association of American Feed Control Officials**, whose Official Publication is *recommended* as the definition guideline for ingredient labelling in Canada. Recommended, not required — and AAFCO is a US body with no Canadian regulatory authority.

So when a bag says it meets AAFCO nutrient profiles, that is a manufacturer's statement against an American voluntary standard. It is meaningful information and it is worth looking for. It is not a Canadian government approval. Ask how the manufacturer substantiated the statement rather than assuming that no testing occurred.

## What has to be on the label

Prepackaged pet food sold at retail in Canada must carry:

- The **common name**, in English and French
- A **metric net quantity** declaration
- The **dealer's name and principal place of business**

Those three are the legal floor, and they come from the Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act, which is the piece the Competition Bureau administers. Notice what is not in them: nothing about what is in the food.

The Bureau’s guide then sets out what a label should carry to be useful and not misleading — an ingredient list in descending order by percentage of weight, a guaranteed analysis, feeding instructions, and a nutritional adequacy statement or intended life stage. Treat that second list as the standard a label is judged against rather than as a separate statute, and read a bag that omits one of them accordingly.

That last one is the most useful line on the bag and the one people skim past. It tells you whether the food is complete and balanced, and for which life stage — growth, adult maintenance, all life stages. A food labelled for intermittent or supplemental feeding should not be chosen as an everyday sole diet without veterinary guidance. Some therapeutic diets have specific veterinary directions; do not reclassify those as ordinary treats.

## How to read the ingredient list without being misled

The Competition Bureau’s guide is that ingredients are listed in descending order by percentage of weight. What it does not say is at which point that weight is taken — the “measured before cooking” explanation you will read everywhere comes from US labelling practice, not from a Canadian source we could find, so we are not going to state it as the Canadian rule.

The practical consequence holds either way, because it is about moisture rather than about which statute applies.

**Moisture distorts the top of the list.** A fresh ingredient and a dried ingredient contain different amounts of water, so their positions alone do not tell you their contribution to the finished diet. First on the list does not mean the greatest protein contribution.

**Splitting distorts the middle.** Where a single ingredient is divided into several named fractions, each fraction is listed on its own and lands further down than the combined ingredient would. “Ingredient splitting” is an interpretive term used by people who read labels, not a legal category. Read the list as a whole rather than fixating on position one.

**A long list is not a worse list.** Much of the tail is vitamins and minerals, which is what "complete and balanced" requires.

The guaranteed analysis has its own trap: the figures are minimums for protein and fat and maximums for fibre and moisture, and they are stated **as fed**. Comparing a tinned food at 78% moisture with a dry food at 10% straight off the label compares mostly water. Both numbers are honest and they are not comparable until moisture is accounted for.

## Comparing wet and dry food: a worked example

Moisture-adjusted comparisons are useful, but guaranteed minimums and maximums are **not exact nutrient measurements**. [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/management-and-nutrition/nutrition-small-animals/dog-and-cat-foods) recommends the manufacturer's average nutrient profile when available.

For an approximate dry-matter percentage, divide the nutrient percentage by (100 minus moisture percentage), then multiply by 100.

## What the marketing words are, and are not

Health claims deserve particular care: Health Canada prohibits unsubstantiated health claims, a manufacturer making a claim is expected to hold adequate and proper testing to support it, and products **cannot use language suggesting they diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat or prevent disease.**

Which is why the interesting phrases on a bag are the ones that stop just short of a claim. "Supports joint health" is not "treats arthritis," and the difference is regulatory rather than nutritional.

Beyond that, be careful with the marketing vocabulary. Words such as premium, super-premium, holistic, gourmet and human-grade appear nowhere in the Competition Bureau’s guide, and we have not found a Canadian definition behind any of them — but “we could not find one” is not the same as “none exists”, and we are not going to dress the first up as the second. What we can say is the part that matters to you: none of those words tells you whether a food is nutritionally appropriate for your animal. The regulated information does, and the nutritional adequacy statement does. What the Bureau does require of any claim is that it be accurate, not misleading, and based on adequate and proper tests. Where a word does have a technical meaning — a named ingredient, a life-stage statement, a percentage — it is doing real work. Where it is an adjective, generally it is not.

## The questions that actually get you somewhere

The label can only tell you so much. Two better sources exist.

**Your veterinary clinic**, which knows your individual animal, its life stage, weight, body condition and any medical reason a diet should change. Nutrition is one of the few subjects where the internet is confidently and loudly wrong in every direction, and where an animal in front of somebody is worth more than any amount of reading.

**The manufacturer**, directly. Companies vary enormously in how readily they will tell you who formulates their diets, what qualifications those people hold, whether they own the plant that makes the food, and whether they have run feeding trials rather than only formulating to a profile. A company that answers those quickly and specifically is telling you something; so is one that does not.

<Checklist title="Worth asking about a food">

- Is it complete and balanced, and for which life stage?
- Which nutrient profile does it state it meets, and how was that established?
- Who formulates the diets, and what are their qualifications?
- Does the company own the manufacturing facility, or is production contracted out?
- Have feeding trials been conducted, or is the food formulated to a profile only?
- Can you provide typical nutrient data on a common basis, so I can compare wet and dry?
- Is this appropriate for my animal's life stage, body condition and any medical condition?
- What does the feeding guide say, and is it a starting point I should adjust?

</Checklist>

## The Canadian practicalities

**Availability is part of the decision.** Before committing, check local stock or realistic delivery options so you can plan replenishment rather than make an unplanned change.

**Bilingual labelling is a requirement here**, which is a small but genuine tell. A product sold at Canadian retail with English-only packaging is worth a second look.

**Cost per day, not cost per bag.** A denser food fed in smaller portions can be cheaper to run than a cheaper bag fed in larger ones. Both the [dog](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) and [cat](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) cost guides make the same point: compare recurring food costs using the appropriate daily amount, not bag price alone.

**Life stage actually changes.** A growing puppy and an adult may need different nutritional profiles. Review the recipe and portion with your veterinarian as age, body condition or health changes. Feeding guides are a starting point calculated for an average animal, and yours is not one.

## The short version

Do not mistake a label statement for Canadian government approval of a diet. Separate packaging requirements, nutritional information and marketing claims, and ask for evidence where the label leaves a gap.

So read the load-bearing parts: the nutritional adequacy statement and life stage, the guaranteed analysis with moisture accounted for, and the ingredient list as a whole rather than by first position. Treat premium, holistic and human-grade as marketing rather than specification.

Then ask your clinic, and ask the manufacturer who formulates their food. Those two answers will tell you more than an hour of reading bags.
