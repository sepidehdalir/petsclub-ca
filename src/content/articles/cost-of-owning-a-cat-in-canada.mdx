Cats are cheaper than dogs. That is true, it is the first thing everyone says, and it is where most cost planning stops — which is why cat owners are more often caught out than dog owners.

This guide quotes no dollar figures, for the same reason [the dog version](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-dog-in-canada) doesn’t: veterinary fees are set independently by each practice, food and litter prices move constantly, and municipal charges differ between adjacent cities. A national average is a number you could plan against and be wrong by a wide margin. What travels is the structure — every category, what actually drives it, and how to build a real figure for your own household in about an hour.

## Where the cat-versus-dog comparison holds, and where it breaks

**Cats genuinely cost less** in the places size determines. Less food. Smaller doses of everything, which matters more than people expect because a lot of veterinary medication is priced by weight. Cheaper boarding, or a visiting sitter rather than a kennel.

**But four things push the other way**, and they are the ones that get missed:

- **Litter is a permanent line item** that dog owners simply do not have. It runs for the whole life of the cat and scales directly with how many cats you have.
- **Cats are usually not singular.** Households acquire a second cat far more readily than a second dog, and almost every category on this page doubles when they do.
- **Cats conceal illness.** A cat is often further into a problem by the time anyone notices, which tends to mean a more involved and more expensive intervention than the equivalent caught early.
- **Indoor cats live a long time.** A long life is the point, and it also means more senior years, and senior years are where the veterinary spending sits.

## One-time setup

- **Adoption fee or purchase price.** A rescue fee frequently bundles spay or neuter, the initial vaccines, deworming, a microchip and FeLV/FIV testing. That changes the comparison with a private sale substantially — compare what is *included*, not the headline number.
- **Spay or neuter**, if it is not already done.
- **Microchip** implantation and the registration that has to follow it.
- **The vaccination series**, which is several appointments over a couple of months rather than one visit — the [kitten vaccination guide](/guides/kitten-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) explains why.
- **Litter boxes**, at one per cat plus one, with a scoop and a mat.
- **A carrier.** Buy a good one. It will be in service for fifteen years and it is the difference between a routine vet visit and an ordeal.
- **Scratching posts and a cat tree.** Not optional, and the cheap short posts get ignored and replaced, which makes them the expensive option.
- **Beds, bowls, a brush, toys.**
- **Municipal licence**, in the Canadian cities that license cats.

## Recurring

- **Food.** The largest ongoing line. The wet-versus-dry decision moves it considerably, and it is a question for your veterinarian rather than a budgeting exercise.
- **Litter.** The line dogs do not have. It scales with the number of cats and with the type you choose, and it is bought forever.
- **Parasite prevention**, where it applies. What a cat actually needs depends on whether it goes outside, whether there are other animals in the house, and where in the country you are — a clinical decision, not a shopping one.
- **Replacement scratching surfaces.** Cardboard is a consumable.
- **Toys**, which are cheap individually and add up over a rotation.

## Annual and irregular

- **Wellness examination**, and boosters at whatever interval the vaccines require.
- **Dental care.** A cleaning under general anaesthetic is a real procedure with anaesthesia, monitoring and often extractions. For many cats it is the largest planned expense of the middle years, and dental disease is common enough in cats that it belongs in a budget rather than in the surprises column.
- **Boarding or a sitter** when you travel. Often cheaper than for a dog — a visiting sitter for a cat is a different service from a kennel — but not free, and it recurs every trip.
- **Replacing the cat tree** when it is shredded or outgrown.
- **Bloodwork**, which becomes routine as a cat ages.

## The expensive realities that are specific to cats

Three categories come up often enough in feline medicine that a cat owner should know the names before they hear them at a clinic. None of this is a prediction about your cat; it is what the budget should be able to absorb.

- **Urinary blockage.** A cat straining in the litter box and producing little or nothing is an emergency, particularly in male cats, and treatment usually means hospitalisation rather than an appointment. It is the single most common reason a cat budget goes from comfortable to not.
- **The conditions of older cats.** Kidney disease and thyroid problems are among the things veterinarians commonly manage in senior cats, and management means ongoing medication, prescription diets and repeated monitoring rather than a single bill.
- **Dental disease.** Common, progressive, and expensive once extractions are involved.

Add the fact that cats hide illness — the reason annual examinations earn their cost in a way that feels abstract right up until they don’t.

## Multi-cat multiplies more than you think

Two cats is not two lots of food and otherwise the same. It is:

- double the litter, and one more box than you have cats
- two sets of vaccines, examinations and dental work
- more boarding, or a longer sitter visit
- and, when something is wrong, frequently an investigation involving both animals, because you often cannot tell which cat is the one with the problem

If you are planning on two, budget for two from the start rather than treating the second as an incremental cost.

## Insurance, or a savings account

Same choice as for any pet: a policy, a dedicated savings account, or both. The one thing specific to cats is timing — a cat that will live eighteen years accumulates a long file, and the cover you can buy narrows with every entry in it.

The whole argument, including the four numbers that actually settle a claim and why the exclusions matter more than the premium, is in the [insurance guide](/guides/pet-insurance-in-canada). Either route is defensible; neither can be started on the day you need it.

## What actually drives the number

Roughly in order:

- **How many cats.** This dominates everything else, and it is the variable most likely to change without a decision being made about it.
- **Indoor or outdoor.** Affects parasite prevention, vaccination profile and injury risk. The [indoor and outdoor guide](/guides/indoor-or-outdoor-cats-in-canada) covers the rest of that decision.
- **Age.** Kittens are expensive at the start, seniors at the end, and the middle years are cheap enough to lull you.
- **Coat.** A long-haired cat may need professional grooming; a short-haired one needs a brush.
- **Breed predispositions.** Some pedigree breeds carry well-documented tendencies toward specific expensive conditions. Ask a veterinarian about the breed *before* choosing it.
- **Where you live.** Fees, licensing and access to emergency care vary between provinces and between cities.

## Building your own number in about an hour

1. **Decide how many cats** you are actually going to have.
2. **Phone three veterinary clinics near you.** Ask what a wellness examination costs, what the kitten vaccination series costs, and what spay or neuter costs. Clinics quote this routinely.
3. **Ask the same clinics** what parasite prevention they would expect an indoor cat in your area to need, and what that runs annually.
4. **Price the litter** you intend to use, per month, times the number of cats.
5. **Price the food** at the daily amount for an adult cat. Cost per bag or per case means nothing; cost per day is the number.
6. **Look up your municipality’s licensing page** to see whether cats are licensed where you are, and what it costs.
7. **Get two or three insurance quotes** for that breed, age and postal code. Even if you decide not to insure, the premium is a useful market estimate of the risk you are choosing to carry.
8. **Price a sitter** for one week, and multiply by how often you travel.

That is a real budget, sourced by you, specific to your city and your household.

## The costs people forget

- **A second cat**, acquired on a Saturday with no budgeting at all.
- **Furniture**, when the scratching provision was inadequate.
- **A prescription diet**, which can move food from the smallest line to one of the largest.
- **Damage deposits and pet-friendly housing.** Renting with animals has its own economics — the [renting guide](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada) covers what to establish before signing.
- **The enrichment a full-time indoor cat needs**, which is modest in money and real in time — see the [enrichment guide](/guides/indoor-cat-enrichment-canadian-homes).
- **End-of-life care.** Palliative treatment, euthanasia and aftercare. The least discussed cost, arriving when nobody is in a position to compare prices.

## The short version

Cats are cheaper than dogs, and the routine monthly cost is genuinely modest. The part that matters is not the routine cost — it is whether an unplanned emergency, or fifteen years of senior care at the end of a long indoor life, would be an inconvenience or a crisis.

Litter and the number of cats are the two variables people never model. Model them, phone three clinics, and you will have a figure that is actually about your household rather than about a national average nobody can source.
