There is no single price for owning a cat across Canada. For a useful reference, the **Ontario Veterinary Medical Association's 2026–27 adult-cat estimate is CAD $3,594 a year**, equivalent to **$299.50 a month** when divided by 12. This is a provincial estimate, not a Canada-wide average or a quote for your cat. Use it to spot missing categories, then replace the allowances with your own prices.

The [OVMA's current cost sheet](https://www.ovma.org/getmedia/343d20ed-e15f-41f2-914d-c24527522f6e/OVMA-CostofCare-Feline.pdf?ext=.pdf), checked October 22, 2026, includes food, litter, veterinary categories, professional dental care, insurance and other essentials. Its inclusion of a procedure does not mean every cat needs it annually. Your veterinary team should advise on your animal's care.

## What that monthly number does, and does not, tell you

Dividing an annual estimate by 12 creates a planning allowance, not a monthly bill or a spending ceiling. One month may involve food and litter; another may include a larger veterinary invoice or holiday care. The published estimate is not a promise about either month.

Ask what is missing from your own household's plan: adoption or purchase, travel to collect the cat, a sitter, boarding, equipment replacement and unexpected treatment. Do not assume an insurance allowance covers every emergency. The OVMA itself says costs vary and other expenses may arise.

Keep four totals separate:

1. **Before arrival:** acquisition, initial equipment and anything not included in the adoption arrangement.
2. **Regular spending:** food, litter, recurring supplies and any insurance premium.
3. **Planned occasional bills:** your clinic's estimate, licensing, holiday care and replacements.
4. **Emergency arrangements:** accessible savings and the payment and coverage details of any policy.

An unknown cost should be marked **not quoted**, not zero. This matters most when a reassuring monthly total leaves out a bill you have not researched yet.

For a normal-year spending estimate, add regular monthly expenses multiplied by 12 to planned occasional bills. For the first year, add one-time setup costs that are not already in those categories. Track contributions to savings separately so that transferring money into a reserve does not get counted again as an expense when you later use it.

### Compare food and litter without misleading shortcuts

A cheaper bag is not necessarily cheaper per month. Compare the amount you actually use, the delivered price and whether a promotional discount disappears on the next order. For food, compare suitable options with your veterinary team; price per kilogram alone does not account for different portions.

For litter, note how long the product lasts in your household before buying a large quantity. A different pack size, number of trays or number of cats can change the calculation. Keep the purchase receipt and revisit the allowance after the first month rather than treating a guess as a measured result.

## First-year costs: avoid buying the same thing twice

Before paying an adoption fee, request an itemised list of included care, identification and supplies, together with the cat's records. The [BC SPCA's adoption page](https://spca.bc.ca/adoption/how-to-adopt/) lists a cat-adoption package that includes spay or neuter surgery and microchip identification. That is one organisation's offer, not a rule for every Canadian shelter. Confirm the current package for the cat you are adopting.

Then write down what remains: a suitable carrier, litter equipment, bowls, resting spaces, scratching surfaces and home preparation. Include tax and shipping in the setup total. Do not assume the most expensive version is necessary or that every item from a previous pet is suitable.

A carrier that cannot accommodate your cat is a poor saving. Use our [cat-carrier checklist](/guides/choosing-a-cat-carrier-in-canada) to compare usable dimensions, access and care instructions. For scratching equipment, the [scratcher buying guide](/guides/choosing-a-cat-scratching-post-in-canada) separates usable surface from overall size.

## A second cat needs a separate calculation

Do not simply halve shared costs or double the first cat's total. Write individual lines for each cat's food, prescribed care, insurance and veterinary estimates. Then identify what can genuinely be shared and what needs additional equipment or space.

Ask a sitter whether the second cat changes the rate. Check whether each cat needs a separate carrier and whether the household needs more feeding, resting or litter resources. Our [indoor-cat enrichment guide](/guides/indoor-cat-enrichment-canadian-homes) covers planning the home around the cats rather than buying a pile of accessories.

## Keep optional purchases out of the essentials total

If you want a puzzle feeder or new toy, give it a separate allowance after required care and supplies. Check whether it suits your cat, the food you use, available space and cleaning routine. ## Review the plan before the next large bill

A monthly allowance is useful only if it matches the payment calendar. If an annual bill falls due next week, spreading its cost over the next 12 months will not pay it on time. Keep due dates beside the amounts and review the plan when your cat's needs or household arrangements change.

For uncertain veterinary costs, ask the clinic for the scope and timing of its estimate. For uncertain insurance coverage, ask the insurer rather than treating a premium as a guarantee. The [veterinary-estimate checklist](/guides/what-veterinary-care-costs-in-canada) and [Canadian pet-insurance guide](/guides/pet-insurance-in-canada) provide questions to take to those conversations.
