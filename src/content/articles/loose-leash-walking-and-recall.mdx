These two are the skills owners want most and train least systematically, and they fail for the same reason: both get tested in the situation where the dog is least able to succeed, long before they have been built in the situations where it could.

A dog that walks beautifully in the hallway and drags you down the street has not been trained badly. It has been trained in the hallway, and then examined on the street.

## The method, and why it is not a matter of taste

Before any of the mechanics: the veterinary behaviour position on how to train is unambiguous, and it is worth stating because a great deal of what circulates contradicts it.

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior’s 2021 position statement is that, based on current scientific evidence, **only reward-based training methods are used for all dog training, including the treatment of behavior problems**. What it says an appropriate trainer should avoid is set out in four categories, and it names them: tools that involve **pain** — choke chains, prong collars, electronic shock collars; **intimidation** — squirt bottles, shaker noise cans, compressed air cans, shouting, staring, or forceful manipulation such as alpha rolls or dominance downs; **physical correction techniques** — leash jerking and physical force; and **flooding**, meaning forced exposure. Its standard for the dog is that the learner must always feel safe and be able to opt out of a training session.

The statement is explicit that difficulty is not a reason to reach for those tools. On animals with challenging behaviour disorders such as aggression, it says they should be treated with humane methods “rather than with a heavy hand”, and that **there are no exceptions to this standard** — a trainer who is stuck should consult another reward-based trainer or refer to a veterinarian or veterinary behaviourist.

The reasoning is practical rather than sentimental: aversive methods cause stress, carry welfare risks, and damage the relationship the training depends on. Reward-based methods are supported by the evidence and are better for the human–animal bond.

For lead walking specifically, this matters because the equipment marketed as a fix for pulling is largely the equipment in that list. A dog that stops pulling because pulling hurts has not learned where to walk. It has learned that the walk is unpleasant.

## Loose-leash walking

### What you are actually teaching

Not "heel." Heel is a precision position for short periods. What you want for a forty-minute walk is looser than that: **the lead stays slack and the dog chooses to stay near you**, with the freedom to sniff and look around.

That distinction matters because trying to hold a formal heel for a whole walk is exhausting for both of you and nobody sustains it.

### The mechanics

**Reinforce the position you want, continuously at first.** Every few steps that the lead is slack, the dog gets something — a piece of food delivered at your leg, on the side you want it. You are not waiting for a mistake to correct; you are paying for the thing you want frequently enough that it becomes the dog's default.

**Deliver the reward in the right place.** Food handed out in front of you teaches forging ahead. Food delivered down at your trouser seam teaches "beside the human is where good things happen." This one detail fixes more pulling than any equipment.

**Stop paying for pulling.** The reason dogs pull is that pulling works — it gets them to the lamp post. So when the lead tightens, forward progress stops. Stand still, or turn and walk a few steps the other way, and resume the moment the lead loosens. This is undramatic and it takes a lot of repetitions, because you are unpicking a habit that has been reinforced hundreds of times.

**Let sniffing be a reward rather than an interruption.** Reaching the interesting patch of grass is one of the most powerful things available to you. Use it: slack lead for ten steps, then release to go and sniff.

**Equipment, honestly.** A well-fitted harness with a front attachment point, or a flat collar, and a fixed-length lead of about two metres. Retractable leads teach a dog that pulling extends the lead, which is the opposite lesson.

### Build it where the dog can succeed

The universal mistake is starting on the pavement outside the front door, which for most dogs is already too hard.

**Separate the exercise walk from the training walk.** Trying to do both at once is why people give up. A dog still needs to get out and empty itself; a fifteen-minute training walk plus a longer decompression walk on a long line is far more productive than forty minutes of frustrated negotiation.

## Recall

Recall is the more important of the two and the one where impatience does the most damage.

### The one rule that protects it

**Coming back must always be worth it, and it must never end the good thing.**

Almost every broken recall traces to a dog that learned the word predicts something it does not want — going home, the lead going on, a bath, being told off for taking too long. If "come" reliably ends the fun, a dog will start weighing whether to answer, and once it starts weighing you have lost the automatic response you were building.

So: pay well when the dog arrives, and then frequently **release it back to what it was doing**. Call, reward, release. Nine times out of ten, coming back should cost the dog nothing.

And never call a dog to something it will dislike. Go and get it instead.

### Never call a recall you cannot back up

This is the discipline that separates a reliable recall from a hopeful one. If you call and the dog does not come, the word has been slightly devalued — and doing that a dozen times teaches the dog that the cue is optional.

Which means a recall gets used only when you are confident of the answer, or when you have a long line on and can make the outcome happen without drama.

### The progression

**Start with a word the dog has no history with.** If "come" has already been shouted across a field forty times without result, it carries that history. Pick a fresh cue and protect it.

**Charge it indoors.** Say the word, the dog gets something excellent, every time, no expectation of distance. You are building an association before you ask for behaviour.

**Add short distance, then a room, then the garden.** Still easy, still paid every time.

**Move onto a long line** — five to ten metres, attached to a harness rather than a collar, for anywhere the dog is not enclosed. This is the stage that most people skip and it is the one that makes off-lead recall possible. The line means you are never in the position of calling and being ignored: you can reel the situation in calmly if you need to, so the cue never gets devalued.

**Increase distraction gradually, one variable at a time.** A quiet field, then a field with a dog visible in the distance, then closer. The order is always distance first, then duration, then distraction.

**Practise the hard version deliberately** — calling away from a smell, from another dog at a distance, mid-play — but only at a difficulty you expect to win at.

### On off-lead

Nothing here produces a guaranteed recall, and any article that promises one is selling something. Recall is a trained behaviour that competes with the environment, and there will be environments that win — a deer, a running child, a dog it desperately wants to meet.

What that means practically: the long line stays on until recall is genuinely solid in an environment like the one you are considering, and the decision to unclip is a risk judgement about that place, that day, that dog. Roads, wildlife, livestock and unfamiliar ground are reasons to keep it on regardless of how good the recall is at home. In [Parks Canada's protected places dogs must be leashed](/guides/travelling-with-a-pet-in-canada), and leash rules elsewhere are municipal.

## The mistakes that cause most of the trouble

- **Calling the dog to end the walk.** The single most efficient way to break a recall. Call, reward, release — several times — and clip the lead on at a random moment that is not a recall.
- **Repeating the cue.** "Come. Come. COME." teaches that the word means nothing until the fourth time. Say it once and make it work.
- **Punishing a slow return.** A dog told off on arrival has learned that arriving is punished. However long it took, the return is always rewarded.
- **Testing rather than training.** Trying it in the busiest place available to see whether it holds. It won't, and you have taught the dog it can decline.
- **Practising only when it matters.** Recall practised twenty times a day in easy situations is what makes it available in the one that counts.
- **Trying to train a dog that is over threshold.** A dog that will not take food is too aroused or too worried to learn — the same signal that governs [socialisation](/guides/puppy-socialisation-checklist). Increase distance and try again.
- **Expecting adolescence to be linear.** Reliability often becomes less consistent for a while, and it is worth knowing how thin the evidence for a schedule is. The best measurement available is Asher and colleagues’ 2020 study, which sampled at roughly five, eight and twelve months and found that at around eight months carers rated their dogs as less trainable than at five or twelve, and that the dogs responded less to a cue from their own carer while responding *better* to the same cue from a stranger. Professional trainers rated the same dogs as more trainable, not less. That was a guide-dog population — German shepherds, golden and Labrador retrievers and crosses — and the authors say the age groupings would need reconsidering for other breeds, so it is not a six-to-eighteen-month timetable your dog is due on. Treat a dip as the test getting harder rather than the training failing: go back a stage and keep paying.

## Where to get help

A group class is worth it for most dogs, and the criteria that matter are whether the class uses reward-based methods, keeps groups small, and gives you something to practise. A trainer who proposes a prong or electronic collar is proposing something the veterinary behaviour position says should not be used.

If a dog is lunging, barking or freezing at other dogs or people rather than simply pulling, that is fear or frustration rather than a lead-walking problem, and it needs a different plan. Start with your own [veterinary clinic](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada), which can rule out pain — a dog that hurts is a dog that reacts — and refer you appropriately. Sudden reluctance to walk is worth an appointment rather than more training, since [joint pain](/guides/arthritis-and-mobility-in-dogs-and-cats) presents as a dog doing less rather than as a limp.

## The short version

Both skills fail for the same reason: they get tested in hard places before they are built in easy ones. Work at a level the dog succeeds at, increase one variable at a time, and go back a stage without treating it as failure.

For the lead, pay frequently, deliver the food at your leg, stop moving when it tightens, and use sniffing as a reward. For recall, protect the cue — never call what you cannot back up, never call to end the fun, and release the dog back to what it was doing most of the time.

Use reward-based methods, because that is the veterinary behaviour position and because the alternative teaches a dog that walks are unpleasant. And keep the long line on longer than you think you need to.
