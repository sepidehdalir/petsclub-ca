Crossing a provincial boundary with an animal in the back of the car changes more than the postal code. It can create legal obligations you did not have on the other side of the line, remove protections you were relying on, and quietly invalidate the arrangements you had made for the worst day.

None of it is difficult. All of it is invisible unless somebody tells you, and nobody does — there is no moment in a move where a form asks about the dog.

This is what actually changes, and when to deal with each part.

## Three layers change at once

The reason moving provinces is more involved than moving across town is that Canadian pet rules sit at three different levels of government, and a move between provinces changes two of them simultaneously.

## The rabies question, which can appear or disappear

This is the one that catches people going east.

**Ontario requires it.** Dogs, cats and ferrets over three months must be vaccinated against rabies under provincial regulation — indoor animals included — with a booster within one year and then every one to three years depending on the product. There are fines for non-compliance.

**British Columbia does not.** The BC Centre for Disease Control recommends rabies vaccination; provincial law does not compel it.

So a household moving from Vancouver to Toronto acquires a legal obligation it did not previously have, for an animal that may never have been vaccinated. A household moving the other way keeps the veterinary recommendation and loses the legal requirement. Neither direction is announced to you.

Some municipalities add their own requirement on top: Ottawa's animal bylaw independently requires dogs and cats three months and over to be currently immunised, which means an Ottawa household satisfies a provincial rule and a municipal one from the same appointment.

Check your destination province, and then your destination municipality. The [puppy](/guides/puppy-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) and [kitten](/guides/kitten-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) vaccination guides set out how the provincial picture varies.

## The tenancy question, which is the expensive one

If you are renting at the other end, this is the item to settle before you sign anything — and it is the one where people most often assume their old rules travel with them.

**Ontario:** a provision in a tenancy agreement prohibiting animals is void under the *Residential Tenancies Act, 2006*. It does not oblige a landlord to select you, and it does not override condominium rules.

**British Columbia:** a tenancy agreement may prohibit pets outright, or restrict their size, kind or number, and a breach can lead to eviction. A pet damage deposit is permitted, capped at half of one month's rent, one deposit however many pets.

**Quebec:** a lessor has the right to refuse animals, and a signed clause binds the tenant, with an exception for an animal that palliates a handicap.

An Ontario tenant moving to British Columbia is moving from a jurisdiction where the clause is void to one where it is enforceable and a deposit is lawful. That is a material change in your negotiating position, and the [renting guide](/guides/renting-with-a-pet-in-canada) covers how to be the applicant a landlord says yes to.

## Licensing does not come with you

Municipal licences are issued by one municipality and generally do not transfer — including across a boundary you might not think of as a move at all.

What varies at the destination is more than the fee: whether cats are licensed, at what age the obligation starts, and what is bundled. Toronto, Ottawa, Calgary and Edmonton all license cats; Edmonton's threshold is six months where several others use three, and its licence includes a microchip. [The comparison is worth reading before you arrive](/guides/pet-licensing-across-canada), because the assumption that licensing is a dog matter is the most common and most expensive mistake a moving cat owner makes.

## Your veterinary relationship, which you have to rebuild

The part people leave until something is wrong.

**Ask for the records before you go, not after.** Your existing practice holds vaccination history, test results, imaging, medications and surgical notes, and getting them transferred is far easier while you are still a current client. Ask how records are requested and whether anything is charged for copying them.

**Register at the other end before you need to.** A practice that already holds the history is worth more than one that is marginally closer, and the wait for a first appointment in some parts of the country is measured in weeks. [How to choose one](/guides/finding-a-veterinarian-in-canada) covers what to ask — including the question that matters most, which is what happens at two in the morning.

**Know the new regulator.** Veterinarians are licensed provincially, by a college in some provinces and a veterinary medical association in others, and the complaints process differs accordingly. You will almost certainly never need this; it costs nothing to know which body it is.

**Get enough medication to cover the gap.** A repeat prescription from a practice you have left, filled in a province where you are not yet registered anywhere, is a problem best avoided by carrying a buffer.

## The microchip registry, which is the ten minutes that matters most

Update it before you leave, not after you arrive.

An animal is most likely to escape during a move — doors propped, movers in and out, an unfamiliar house, a frightened animal that does not yet know where home is. That is precisely the window in which the registry still lists your old address and old phone number.

Add a second contact who will be reachable during the move itself, since you may be driving. And remember what the Canadian Veterinary Medical Association is explicit about: a chip only works when somebody scans it, and not all scanners read all chips, so a collar tag with a current mobile number remains the layer that needs no equipment at all. [If the worst happens, the first hour is what counts.](/guides/when-a-pet-goes-missing-in-canada)

## A sequence that works

<Checklist title="Before, during and after">

- **Six weeks out:** check the destination province's rabies position, and the destination municipality's licensing rules
- **Six weeks out:** if renting, establish the destination province's tenancy position before you sign
- **Four weeks out:** book any vaccination the new province requires, allowing time for it to be in effect
- **Four weeks out:** request your records, and ask your current clinic to recommend or transfer to a practice at the destination
- **Two weeks out:** register with a new practice and book a routine appointment for shortly after arrival
- **Two weeks out:** fill prescriptions with a buffer
- **One week out:** update the microchip registry with the new address, phone and a second contact
- **Moving day:** animal shut in one closed room with a sign on the door, or boarded elsewhere entirely
- **On arrival:** one room first, as with a new arrival — expand from there
- **First fortnight:** licence in the new municipality, update the collar tag, confirm the new after-hours destination

</Checklist>

## The journey and the first week

Getting there is its own project — restraint in the car, never leaving an animal in a parked vehicle, accommodation policies in writing, and a clinic identified near the destination before you need one. All of that is in the [travel guide](/guides/travelling-with-a-pet-in-canada), and a cross-country move is the case it was written for.

At the other end, resist the instinct to give a nervous animal the run of the new house immediately. One room, set up with familiar things, and let them expand at their own pace — the same principle that works for a [new kitten](/guides/bringing-home-a-kitten-first-30-days) works for a cat that has just lost its entire territory. Cats in particular should stay indoors for several weeks after a move regardless of their previous arrangement, because a cat that goes out too early in an unfamiliar place is a cat looking for the old house.

Expect a settling period measured in weeks. Appetite, litter habits and sleep can all wobble. What is worth a call rather than patience is anything that persists, or any cat that stops using the litter box, since that is as likely to be medical as behavioural.

## The short version

Two of the three layers of Canadian pet rules change when you cross a provincial line, and neither of them tells you.

Check the destination province's rabies position — Ontario requires vaccination, British Columbia does not. Check the destination province's tenancy law before you sign, because a no-pets clause that was void where you lived may bind where you are going. Licence in the new municipality, since your old one does not transfer and the rules on cats may not match.

Then do the ten minutes that matters most: update the microchip registry before moving day, not after. A move is when animals get out.
