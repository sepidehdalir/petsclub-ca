A kitten asks less of your calendar than a puppy and much more of your house. There is no toilet training, no 3 a.m. trip outside in February, and very little that has to happen on a deadline. What there is instead is a set of decisions about rooms, boxes and furniture that quietly determine how the next fifteen years go.

Most of what goes wrong with cats — litter box problems, biting hands, a cat that vanishes when the doorbell rings — traces back to the first month. Almost none of it is hard to get right if you know it is coming.

## Before the kitten arrives: set up one room

The single most useful thing you can do is resist giving the kitten the whole house.

Cats settle by taking control of territory, and a kitten turned loose into a four-bedroom home has too much to hold. One room — a spare bedroom, an office, even a bathroom — that the kitten owns completely for the first days is not a restriction. It is the thing that makes the rest of the house safe to expand into later.

That room needs:

- **A litter box**, well away from the food.
- **Food and water**, and water not right beside the food.
- **Somewhere to hide** — a cardboard box on its side, a covered bed, the space under a chair.
- **Something to climb**, even if it is only a chair next to a windowsill.
- **A scratching post**, tall enough for a growing cat to stretch up.
- **A bed**, and the open carrier.

Also before arrival:

- **Book the first veterinary appointment**, and ask what to bring.
- **Settle the insurance question**, or at least understand the timing — a condition written into a file stops being insurable. The [cat cost guide](/guides/cost-of-owning-a-cat-in-canada) lays out insuring versus saving.
- **Kitten-proof at cat height, which is everywhere.** Blind and curtain cords, electrical cables, the gap behind the fridge, the open washing machine and dryer, and anything small and swallowable — hair elastics, elastic bands, and above all string, thread, tinsel and ribbon.
- **Check your houseplants, and every bouquet that comes through the door.** True lilies (*Lilium*) and daylilies (*Hemerocallis*) are the ones that matter, and the exposure routes are broader than people expect: eating any part of the plant, licking pollen off a paw or a face, or drinking water out of the vase. The consequence is acute kidney injury, and it can be fatal. The two details that make this worth acting on before the kitten arrives are that a very small exposure is enough, and that the signs — vomiting, going off food, drinking and urinating more, hiding — may not appear for two or three days, by which point treatment is far less likely to work. If you cannot identify a plant, keep it out of reach or out of the house.
- **Check the windows and the balcony.** An insect screen will not hold a cat. The [indoor and outdoor guide](/guides/indoor-or-outdoor-cats-in-canada) covers balconies and screens properly.

## Day one: the carrier, the car, the room

Take the carrier into the room, open the door, and leave. Do not tip the kitten out, do not lift it out, do not sit and stare at the door. Some kittens stroll out in thirty seconds. Some take four hours. Both are normal.

Show them the litter box once, gently, and then leave it alone. Keep the household quiet. The visitors can wait a week.

## Days one to three: the hiding phase is normal, and brief

Expect a kitten behind or under something. Do not pull it out. Being dragged from a hiding place teaches a cat that hiding places are not safe, which is the opposite of what you want.

What works is dull and reliable: go into the room, sit on the floor, and do something else. Read, work, scroll. Ignore the kitten. Put food down on a schedule and leave it. Within a couple of days curiosity almost always beats caution, and the kitten comes to you — which is a much better first contact than one you initiated.

## The litter box, which you want to get right the first time

More cats lose their homes over litter box problems than over anything else, and most of those problems are setup problems rather than cat problems.

- **Number: one per cat, plus one.** Yes, even for a single kitten. Two.
- **Size: bigger than looks necessary.** A cat should fit with room to turn. Kittens need low sides they can climb over; the box they grow into can come later.
- **Uncovered, to start.** Hoods trap odour in — for the cat, not for you.
- **Location: quiet, easy to reach, with a way out.** Not beside the furnace or the washing machine, not in a cupboard, not at the end of a corridor a resident cat can block, and never next to the food.
- **Litter: fine-grained and unscented.** Most cats prefer it, and scented litter is designed for the human. Start with whatever the shelter or breeder used and change it gradually if you want something else.
- **Scoop daily.** Every day.

If a kitten that was using the box stops, that is information, not naughtiness, and the first call is to a veterinarian rather than to a pet shop.

## Food, water, and the milk question

Keep feeding whatever the kitten was already eating for the first week or so, then transition over at least a week by mixing increasing amounts of the new food in. An abrupt switch on top of a house move is how you get a week of diarrhoea.

Kittens eat little and often, and they need feeding several times a day. How much and how often is worth asking your veterinarian at the first appointment, because it changes fast as they grow.

Put the water somewhere other than beside the food bowl. A lot of cats drink more from a wide, shallow bowl than a deep narrow one, and many prefer moving water. And do not give cow’s milk — most cats past kittenhood do not digest it well, and the “saucer of milk” image has caused a great deal of avoidable diarrhoea.

## Handling, the carrier, and the first vet visit

**Leave the carrier out permanently.** Not in a cupboard. Out, in a room the cat uses, with a blanket in it, as a piece of furniture. A cat that sleeps in its carrier is a cat you can take to a veterinarian without a forty-minute wrestling match, every year, for its whole life. This is the highest-return five minutes in this article.

**Practise handling** for a minute or two a day: touch the paws, press a pad gently to extend a claw, look in the ears, lift a lip, run a brush over the back. Brief, calm, followed by something good.

**At the first appointment**, bring whatever records came with the kitten. Ask what the vaccination plan is and when the series finishes — the [kitten vaccination guide](/guides/kitten-vaccination-schedule-in-canada) explains the shape so the conversation is easier to follow — plus [parasite prevention](/guides/parasite-prevention-for-pets-in-canada) for where you live, when to think about [spaying or neutering](/guides/spaying-and-neutering-in-canada), whether the kitten has been tested for FeLV and FIV, and what the after-hours arrangement is.

## Play, and the one mistake almost everyone makes

**Never play with a kitten using your hands.** It is charming at eight weeks and it is a problem at eight months, and it is far harder to undo than to avoid. Hands are for feeding and stroking. Toys are for chasing.

Two short wand-toy sessions a day is the routine to build. Move the toy like something trying to escape — away from the cat, around corners, disappearing under a blanket — rather than waving it in their face. Let the kitten actually catch it at the end, and then feed a meal. Hunt, catch, eat, sleep is the sequence a cat is built around, and running it deliberately settles a kitten better than almost anything else.

Rotate the toys and put most of them away between sessions. A toy left on the floor for a month is furniture.

## Socialisation for cats is real, and it happens early

A kitten’s primary socialisation period is earlier and shorter than a puppy’s — much of it is finished before you ever meet the animal, which is why how a kitten was raised matters so much.

What you can still do in your month: handle them daily, let them meet a variety of calm people, run the vacuum while something good happens at a distance, practise the carrier, get them used to a brush and to having claws extended, and let them experience ordinary household noise rather than a silent house that makes every sound an event.

## Expanding the territory

Around the end of the first week, open the door of the base camp room and let the kitten decide. Do not carry them out to show them the living room. Leave the original room set up — box, food, hiding place — for at least another couple of weeks, so there is always somewhere to retreat to.

If there is a resident cat or dog, the introduction is a separate project measured in weeks, not days: scent first through a closed door, then feeding on either side of it, then brief supervised visual contact through a barrier, then supervised time together. Rushing it is the most common way to create a problem that lasts years. This is a good question for the [community](/community/kittens), where people have done it recently.

## The paperwork Canadians forget

- **Municipal licensing.** Some Canadian cities license cats the way they license dogs — Toronto and Calgary both do, indoor cats included, and Calgary's threshold is three months of age. Check your own municipality’s animal bylaw rather than assuming yours does not.
- **Microchip registration**, in your name, with a current address and phone number. Implanting and registering are two separate steps and the second is the one people miss.
- **Insurance**, if you are buying it, before there is a medical history.
- **Records**, photographed and kept.

## The short version

Give a kitten one room, then let them take the rest of the house on their own schedule. Get the litter boxes right on day one, because retrofitting a solution is much harder than starting with two uncovered boxes in sensible places. Leave the carrier out for the next decade. Play with toys, never hands.

Everything else you can learn as you go. A cat is a patient teacher, right up until it isn’t.
