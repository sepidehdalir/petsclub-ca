Most kitten vaccination advice online is either American, written for breeders, or a single table with no explanation of why it looks the way it does. This is the background: what the vaccines are for, why it takes several appointments, what changes the answer in Canada, and which questions are worth asking at the clinic.

<Note title="What this is and is not">

animal, the specific product, where you live, whether the cat will go outside,

</Note>

## Why it is a series

A kitten absorbs antibodies from its mother’s first milk. Those antibodies protect it early — and while they are present, they also stop a vaccine from taking effect.

They fade, but on a timetable nobody can observe from outside. It differs between litters and between kittens in the same litter. So the series is not one dose repeated for emphasis; it is a set of attempts spaced a few weeks apart, arranged so that whenever that individual kitten becomes able to respond, a dose lands in the window. The final dose — given at an age by which maternal antibodies have almost always gone — carries most of the weight.

The consequence worth remembering: **a kitten part-way through the series is part-way through the process.** One vaccine is not protection, and it does not change where it is safe to take them.

Dog owners will recognise all of this; the same logic drives the [puppy vaccination series](/guides/puppy-vaccination-schedule-in-canada).

## Core and non-core, for cats

**Core** vaccines are recommended for essentially every cat. For cats in Canada that generally means:

- **Feline panleukopenia** — a feline parvovirus, and a serious one.
- **Feline herpesvirus-1**, the cause of feline viral rhinotracheitis.
- **Feline calicivirus.**

Those three are usually given together in a single injection, which appears on paperwork as **FVRCP**. Ask which product your clinic uses and write it down.

**Rabies** is also core, and in some provinces it carries legal weight in addition to the medical case.

**Feline leukaemia virus (FeLV) is core too — for kittens.** This is the part most owners have not heard, and a lot of older advice online gets it wrong. Both the AAHA/AAFP feline guidelines and WSAVA classify FeLV vaccination as core for every cat up to and including one year of age, not as a lifestyle option. The reasoning is that kittens are far more susceptible than adult cats to lasting infection and death if they are exposed, and that a kitten's future is not yet decided — the cat you intend to keep strictly indoors may escape, may acquire a housemate, or may move house. After the first year it becomes a lifestyle decision, recommended for cats with outdoor access or living with cats that go out. That change in status with age is the whole point, and it is worth an explicit conversation rather than an assumption.

**Non-core** vaccines depend on the cat’s circumstances:

- **Chlamydia felis and Bordetella** are situational, and come up mainly in multi-cat environments.

**Testing before vaccinating** is standard practice: kittens are commonly tested for FeLV and FIV, particularly if their history is unknown. If your kitten came from a shelter this may already have been done — ask, and get it in writing.

## The shape of a typical schedule

A shape, not a prescription. Your clinic’s version may reasonably differ in several places.

- **Around 6–8 weeks** — first FVRCP, and no earlier than six weeks. Often given by the breeder or rescue before the kitten comes home, which is why their records matter.
- **Every 2–4 weeks after that** — the series continues. Two weeks is the minimum gap and four the maximum; if more than six weeks passes between doses, the series may need restarting.
- **No earlier than 16 weeks** — the final dose of the kitten series.
- **Rabies** — timing set by provincial or municipal rules rather than preference. In Ontario the legal trigger is three months of age.
- **FeLV** — two doses, three to four weeks apart, during the kitten period, then a booster twelve months after the last kitten dose. Annually after that only for cats at ongoing risk.
- **Around six months** — an additional FVRCP dose. This is the recent change: the AAHA/AAFP guidelines added a dose at six months to close the gap for kittens whose maternal antibodies were still interfering at the end of the series, and WSAVA advises revaccination at or after 26 weeks rather than waiting for the twelve-month visit.
- **After that** — intervals that depend on the vaccine, the specific product, the cat’s lifestyle and local law. Not everything is annual, and not everything is three-yearly.

Three things to take from that. The series finishes **later than most people expect** — the sixteen-week dose is not a formality. There is now a six-month appointment that older schedules do not show, so ask whether your clinic has adopted it. And the intervals afterwards are set by the product and the cat, so a schedule copied from a friend in another province may not fit yours.

## Rabies is a legal question too

Rabies vaccination in Canada is regulated provincially, and some municipalities add requirements of their own. There is no national rule, and the provinces genuinely differ.

**Ontario** requires it: cats, dogs and ferrets over three months of age must be vaccinated, and the regulation makes no exception for a cat that never goes outside. **British Columbia** does not require it — the BC Centre for Disease Control recommends it, but no provincial law compels it. Rules elsewhere can attach to the animal’s age, to licensing, or to both.

It also surfaces in places people do not anticipate: licensing a cat where cats are licensed, boarding, moving between provinces, crossing the border, and — most importantly — what happens if your cat is exposed to rabies or bites or scratches someone badly enough to be reported. A cat with current, documented rabies vaccination and one without are in materially different positions afterwards. In Alberta, an unvaccinated cat assessed as exposed may face a quarantine of three to six months, decided by the province's public health veterinarian; in Ontario a cat involved in a biting incident is confined and observed for ten days whatever its vaccination status.

Check what applies in your province and municipality, and keep the certificate.

## “But my cat never goes outside”

This is the most common objection and it deserves a straight answer rather than a dismissal.

An indoor-only cat genuinely has a lower exposure risk than one that roams, and that difference is exactly what a lifestyle-based conversation with your veterinarian is for. But indoor-only is not the same as zero risk, for reasons that are specific and mundane:

- **Cats get out.** Doors, movers, contractors, a torn screen, a fire alarm. The cat that has never been outside is the one least equipped to deal with being outside.
- **Bats get in.** Bats find their way into Canadian houses, and a bat in a room with a cat is a rabies exposure question that a public health unit will take seriously. The Public Health Agency of Canada makes the point that a bat's teeth and claws are small enough that a bite or scratch may leave nothing you can see — so “there was no contact” is not something you can be confident about. This is where the vaccination status stops being abstract: it is the difference between a booster and a months-long quarantine.
- **Life changes.** A new cat joins the household. You move. You need to board. Boarding facilities, catteries and many veterinary clinics require proof of vaccination, and the day you need it is not the day to start a series.

None of this means every indoor cat needs every vaccine. It means the decision belongs to a discussion about your specific household, not to a rule of thumb.

## Where the injection goes, and why

You may notice that a veterinarian gives feline vaccines in particular locations, often low on a limb, and records exactly which vaccine went where. There are established reasons in feline medicine for the choice of site and for that record-keeping.

It is a fair question to ask, and clinics are used to answering it. Ask your veterinarian why they vaccinate where they do — it is a better use of the appointment than most of the questions people feel awkward about asking.

## Titres, and spacing

Antibody titre testing measures whether a cat carries antibodies to particular diseases, and some owners discuss it with their veterinarian when adult boosters come round. It has a narrower reach than people assume: WSAVA supports serological testing in cats for panleukopenia, and not for the other two components of FVRCP. So a titre can tell you something useful about one disease and nothing about the others in the same injection. And where rabies vaccination is required by law, a titre does not stand in for it.

Spreading vaccines across more appointments is a conversation to have with the clinic rather than a plan to build alone. Spacing changes the timing of protection, and timing is the entire point of a kitten series.

## Keep the record

Photograph whatever card the breeder, rescue or clinic gives you.

You will be asked for it when you board the cat, move provinces, travel, buy insurance, license the animal where cats are licensed, or register with a new clinic. A lost vaccination history means repeating parts of it.

<Checklist title="Worth asking at the first appointment">

- Which vaccines is my kitten getting today, and which are core at this age versus lifestyle later on?
- When is the next dose due, and do you give the six-month FVRCP dose?
- Is rabies vaccination required by law here, or recommended?
- Has this kitten been tested for FeLV and FIV, and should it be?
- FeLV is core for the first year — what do you recommend after that for a cat that stays indoors, and would a second cat change it?
- Why do you vaccinate where you do, and what should I watch for at the site?
- What should I watch for this afternoon, and what number do I call after hours?

</Checklist>

## The short version

The series exists because nobody can see when a kitten becomes able to respond, so it takes several attempts and the last one — no earlier than sixteen weeks — is the one that counts. FVRCP and rabies are core, and so is FeLV for the first year, which is the part most owners are not told. Ask whether your clinic gives the six-month dose. And find out whether rabies vaccination is law where you live or a recommendation, because in Canada it is one or the other depending on the province.

“Indoor only” is a real input to that decision rather than the end of it. Cats get out, bats get in, and households change.
