There is one fact that reorganises everything else in this article, and most people learn it on the worst possible day.

**Most evacuation shelters will only accept service animals.**

That is the Government of Canada's own guidance, and it means the plan almost everyone has — get out, go to the shelter, sort it out there — does not work if you have a dog or a cat. The plan has to be a different one, and it has to be made now, because the thing about an evacuation order is that it does not come with an afternoon to think.

## Start with the question the shelter answer forces

Where does your animal go?

Not in principle. Specifically, as a written list, in the order you would try it. Public Safety Canada's guidance is to make exactly this list in advance, and the categories it suggests are the right ones:

- **Friends or family outside your immediate area** — far enough that the same event has not displaced them too
- **Hotels and motels that accept animals**, including ones that relax their rules during emergencies
- **Boarding kennels and catteries**
- **Veterinary clinics**
- **Animal shelters and humane societies**

Phone two or three of them now, while nothing is happening, and ask what their arrangements are during an emergency. Write down names and numbers. A list of five options where three are stale is still vastly better than no list, because it means you are making calls rather than searching.

And keep the list somewhere that does not depend on your phone having power or signal.

## The 72-hour standard, and what it means with animals

The Canadian standard for emergency kits is supplies for **at least three days — 72 hours** — whether you evacuate or shelter in place. That standard applies to every member of the household, and Public Safety Canada is explicit that pets are included in it.

The number that surprises people is water. The federal guidance gives **4 litres per day for an average dog and 1 litre per day for an average cat.** Over 72 hours, a two-dog household is carrying roughly 24 litres for the dogs alone, before anybody human has had a drink. That is a real volume, and it is the reason a pet emergency kit needs to be assembled rather than improvised.

<Checklist title="The pet emergency kit, per federal guidance">

- **A sturdy crate or carrier** for each animal — the single most important item, and the one that decides whether an animal can be moved at all
- **A strong leash or harness**, and a collar with an ID tag
- **Food and water for at least 72 hours** — 4L per day per average dog, 1L per day per average cat
- **Bowls**, and a can opener if you use tinned food
- **Newspaper, paper towels and plastic bags** for hygiene
- **Litter and a litter box or tray**
- **Medications**, with the dosage written down and your veterinarian's contact details
- **Your pet's file**: recent photographs, emergency numbers, contact details for people who could house the animal, copies of licences, and vaccination records
- **A pet first-aid kit**
- **A blanket and a familiar toy**

</Checklist>

Two additions worth making beyond the federal list, both of which come from how these situations actually unfold. Put a **written description of each animal** in the file — species, breed, sex, weight, distinctive markings, microchip number — because a photograph on a dead phone is not a description. And add **a second person's contact details** who could collect or house the animal if you cannot get home.

## The carrier is the plan

More evacuations fail on transport than on anything else, and the reason is always the same: the carrier is in the basement behind the Christmas decorations, and there is no time.

Leave it out. Open, with bedding in it, as a piece of furniture the animal already uses. This does two jobs — it is findable in the dark, and it stops being an object your cat associates exclusively with bad days. It is the same argument the [emergency vet guide](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada) makes about the worst night, and it is the same carrier.

For a large dog that cannot walk, or will not load, have a plan and a second person. For a cat that hides when the doorbell goes, know where it hides, because a cat that has vanished under a bed during a wildfire evacuation is a genuine and common problem.

Practise it once. Load everyone into the car and drive around the block. You will find out what you are missing while it costs you nothing.

## Identification, because animals get out in emergencies

Evacuations separate animals from households at a much higher rate than ordinary life does — doors open, fences down, unfamiliar places, and frightened animals that bolt.

Three layers, each of which fails differently:

- **A collar tag with a mobile number.** Works with no equipment, no clinic and no institution.
- **A microchip with current registration.** Works only when somebody scans it — and the Canadian Veterinary Medical Association is explicit that not all scanners read all chips, because several RFID frequencies are in use.
- **A municipal licence tag**, where your city issues one, which is a second traceable number.

Update the chip registry now rather than during an evacuation, and add a second contact. If an animal does get out, [the first hour matters more than the next three days](/guides/when-a-pet-goes-missing-in-canada), and the search looks very different depending on whether you have lost a dog or a cat.

## Leaving early is the whole strategy

Every experienced version of this advice converges on one point: the households that do well are the ones that left before they had to.

An evacuation order compresses everything. An hour of warning is enough to load animals, kit and file if the kit exists and the carriers are out. It is not enough to find a hotel that takes two dogs, locate a carrier, catch a cat that has understood something is wrong, and fill a prescription.

If you are in an area under a warning rather than an order, that is the window. Take the animals out first if you can — to the friend on the list, to the boarding kennel — even if you are staying. It is far easier to bring them back than to retrieve them later.

## If you genuinely cannot take them

This is the section nobody wants and everybody should read once.

Never leave an animal loose outside to fend for itself, and never leave one tethered. Leave them in the house with as much water as you can provide — several containers, in more than one room, in things that cannot be knocked over — and dry food available.

Leave a **notice on the door** stating how many animals are inside, what species, where in the house they usually hide, and your contact number. Some jurisdictions run pet-rescue arrangements during evacuations and that notice is what they work from.

Tell somebody. An emergency services contact, a neighbour who has left, the boarding kennel you called. An animal nobody knows about is an animal nobody looks for.

## Sheltering in place, and the smoke problem

Not every emergency is an evacuation. Wildfire smoke in particular is a shelter-in-place event that now recurs across much of the country.

The measures that protect you protect the animals: windows and doors closed, and a portable air cleaner with a HEPA filter in the room where everyone actually is. Read the Air Quality Health Index — 7 and above is the high-risk band — and cut exertion rather than only duration, because a sniffing walk asks far less of the lungs than a run. The [summer heat guide](/guides/summer-heat-safety-for-dogs-in-canada) covers reading the AQHI for an animal, and is clear that no threshold exists specifically for them, so the human bands are being used as a conservative proxy.

A power failure in winter is the other version of this. An animal that is fine in a heated house is a different animal in an unheated one, and the [winter guide](/guides/winter-dog-care-in-canada)'s point about coat, size, age and health applies indoors too.

<VetNote>
Contact a veterinary clinic if an animal has been exposed to smoke and is
coughing, retching, breathing with visible effort, or unusually lethargic —
breathing difficulty is on every veterinary list of things warranting
immediate attention, and smoke is not a reason to discount it. The same
applies to any animal that has been in flood water, exposed to unknown
chemicals, or injured during an evacuation. Bring medications and the written
dosage list, since a clinic that has never seen your animal is working from
what you can tell them. This describes what to look for so you know when to
call; it does not diagnose or treat anything.
</VetNote>

## Where this fits

An emergency kit is not a separate project from the rest of pet ownership — it is mostly the same folder, carrier and identification the [emergency vet guide](/guides/emergency-vet-visits-in-canada) already asks you to assemble, stored in a way that survives leaving the house. If you have done that, you are most of the way here.

The genuinely additional work is the destination list and the 72 hours of water. Both take an evening.

## The short version

Most evacuation shelters take service animals only, so the plan cannot be "we'll go to the shelter." Make a written list of where the animals could actually go, and phone two of the options now.

Build the 72-hour kit, and take the water seriously — 4 litres a day per dog, 1 per cat, is a volume you have to have already bought. Leave the carrier out where you can reach it in the dark. Update the microchip registry and put a tag on the collar, because evacuations separate animals from families.

And when there is a warning rather than an order, that is the moment to move the animals. Everything is easier an hour early and nearly impossible an hour late.
